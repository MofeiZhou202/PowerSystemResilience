\title{Trustworthy AI-Assisted Modeling and Analysis of
Converter-Dominated Hybrid AC/DC Distribution Systems}
\author[xjtu]{High-Resilience Power \& Energy Systems Group}
\affiliation[xjtu]{organization={School of Electrical Engineering,
Xi'an Jiaotong University},city={Xi'an},country={China}}
\begin{abstract}
Converter-dominated hybrid AC/DC distribution systems are increasingly used to
integrate distributed generation, energy storage, electric vehicles, DC loads,
and networked microgrids. Their analysis frequently requires the conversion of
heterogeneous engineering data and is therefore a natural application for
artificial intelligence (AI). However, a successfully converted model or a
converged power flow does not ensure that the resulting equations retain the
authored network topology, converter controls, or device identities. This study
develops a trustworthy AI-assisted modeling framework based on
semantics-preserving projection from detailed engineering models to a canonical
hybrid AC/DC nodal representation. The method establishes explicit
correspondence between engineering devices and nodal equations, classifies
converter control roles, verifies island reference conditions, and reconstructs
calculated quantities at the device level. AI-proposed modifications are first
applied to an isolated model and are adopted only after the physical,
topological, and numerical conditions have been satisfied. The framework is
evaluated using seven public GridLAB-D distribution feeders with disclosed
AC/DC extensions, 1575 fixed-seed model variants, stress sweeps, three-phase
studies, external-engine comparisons, ablation studies, and constrained AI
modification tests. All 1050 structural faults are detected and localized,
while none of the 175 admissible permutations is rejected. In contrast,
convergence-based screening misses converter-terminal, DC-voltage-support, and
efficiency faults. The results also show that physically plausible but
incorrect setpoints require telemetry or operator confirmation. The proposed
framework provides a verification layer for reliable AI-assisted analysis of
converter-dominated distribution systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Distribution networks are evolving from predominantly passive AC feeders into
converter-dominated hybrid AC/DC systems. Photovoltaic generation, battery
storage, electric-vehicle charging, data-center demand, DC microgrids, and
power-electronic transformers all increase the proportion of electricity that
is processed through converters before reaching the final load. Compared with
a conventional radial feeder, the resulting system contains several voltage
levels, bidirectional AC/DC interfaces, and multiple devices capable of
regulating AC voltage, DC voltage, active power, or reactive power. These
features improve controllability but make the relation between an engineering
model and its power-flow equations more difficult to establish.

The operational benefits of hybrid distribution systems have motivated a broad
range of recent studies. Hybrid AC/DC reconstruction has been investigated for
electric-vehicle integration~\cite{yu2023ev}; mobile storage has been coordinated
with outage restoration~\cite{zhang2023outage}; and soft open points have been
used to enlarge renewable dispatchable regions~\cite{li2024dispatchable}.
Related studies consider converter coordination, voltage regulation, network
reconfiguration, and resilience enhancement. Their optimization and control
models generally assume that the network equations have already been assembled
from an internally consistent description. This assumption is reasonable for a
single benchmark prepared by the analyst, but it becomes restrictive when the
same study must combine utility data, public feeder models, manufacturer data,
and automatically generated modifications.

The range of physical applications is already broad. Recent work includes
reliability-aware topology planning~\cite{wang2022planning}, distribution
reconfiguration with microgrid formation~\cite{wu2022reconfiguration},
integrated and reconfigurable AC/DC building microgrids
~\cite{yu2020architecture}, and forecasting-aided state estimation
~\cite{huang2022state}. At the converter level, capacity management
~\cite{heidari2022capacity}, stability-oriented control
~\cite{khosravi2023stability}, multi-terminal energy control
~\cite{xiao2021multiterminal}, and unbalanced operation of five-terminal
MV/LV systems~\cite{xiao2022five} have been investigated in detail. The
diversity of these studies reinforces the need for a modeling method that does
not depend on one feeder size, one converter topology, or one control mode.

Recent reviews of hybrid AC/DC distribution optimization organize the field
around nodal formulations, converter-loss representations, operational
objectives, and solution methods~\cite{altorok2024opf}. Universal AC/DC branch
models have further reduced the differences between line representations in
joint optimization~\cite{shahbazi2025branch}, while energy-router formulations
have extended coordinated operation to networks with several conversion
interfaces~\cite{zhu2024router}. At the distribution level, reviews of hybrid
AC/DC planning, energy management, control, and protection show that converter
mode selection is central to both normal and disturbed operation
~\cite{abdelwanis2024review}. These studies substantially advance physical
analysis after a network model has been formed. They do not, however, make the
conversion from heterogeneous device descriptions to the adopted equations an
explicit object of assessment. Consequently, a valid formulation can still be
applied to an incomplete or incorrectly associated engineering model.

Distribution-system descriptions are inherently heterogeneous. Geographic and
asset databases emphasize location, ownership, and equipment ratings; planning
models emphasize impedances and aggregated demand; and OpenDSS or GridLAB-D
models may include phase connections, controls, protection devices, and
technology-specific objects. The same transformer, switch, converter, or load
can consequently have different names, units, and terminal descriptions in
different data sources. Conversion between these representations is not merely
a syntactic operation. It determines which terminals are retained, how ideal
connections are treated, whether disconnected islands participate in the
calculation, and how a calculated voltage or power is associated with the
original device.

Digital-twin research has highlighted this modeling problem. Learning-based
methods have been used to recover distribution-network structures and operating
patterns~\cite{hua2023dt}, while experimentally validated microgrid studies have
combined physical and virtual representations~\cite{sifat2025dt}. The accuracy
of a digital representation nevertheless depends on more than the agreement of
selected outputs. A model may reproduce a voltage profile while omitting a
converter terminal, assigning an incorrect control role, or aggregating devices
in a manner that prevents the calculated result from being returned to the
physical asset. These errors are especially important in hybrid AC/DC systems,
where the converter role determines both the governing equations and the source
that balances losses.

Artificial intelligence provides a new means of preparing and modifying such
models. Large language models have been applied to building-energy model
generation~\cite{jiang2024eplusllm} and to state-estimation assistance on a
2135-node distribution feeder~\cite{li2026llm}. Reinforcement-learning
applications in AC, DC, and hybrid microgrids are also increasing
~\cite{nasir2025rlreview}. These studies demonstrate that AI can interpret
engineering instructions and select analysis operations. However, language
plausibility and predictive accuracy do not establish that a modified network
remains physically meaningful. A proposed change may refer to a nonexistent
terminal, remove the only DC-voltage-regulating device, or alter an aggregated
quantity whose physical origin is ambiguous. A nonlinear calculation can still
converge after some of these errors because the assembled equations describe a
different, incomplete system.

The same distinction appears in recent AI studies. Reviews of AI-based
hierarchical microgrid control emphasize forecasting, secondary control,
energy management, and coordinated decision making~\cite{gutierrez2024ai},
whereas recent assessments of large language models in smart grids focus on
information access, operational assistance, and the risks of unverified
responses~\cite{madani2025llm,ruan2024security,zhang2025llmreview}. These
applications require reliable physical models but generally evaluate the AI
output rather than the device-to-equation transformation on which subsequent
analysis depends. The present work addresses this preceding modeling stage:
AI may interpret and propose an engineering change, while acceptance is
determined by the electrical model and the calculated quantities are obtained
from the power-system equations. Related concerns about whether an apparently
interpretable AI result is actually trustworthy have also been reported for
energy forecasting~\cite{liao2024xai}.

Existing model checks address only part of this problem. Data validation can
identify invalid identifiers, ranges, and terminal references, but it does not
necessarily determine whether the converter controls close the physical degrees
of freedom of every energized island. Power-flow convergence verifies a root of
the assembled equations, but not the correspondence between those equations and
the authored devices. Model reduction and network equivalencing preserve
selected electrical quantities, yet the reverse association from the reduced
network to the original assets is often outside the formulation. Finally,
general AI reliability tests quantify the quality of generated text or selected
operations, whereas power-system analysis requires an independent physical
assessment of every proposed model modification.

This distinction also separates the present problem from anomaly or outlier
detection. Anomaly detection evaluates whether a value is statistically
unusual relative to measurements or historical data. The present study asks
whether the model is internally consistent even when every numerical value is
plausible. A 10\% load increase may be statistically reasonable and physically
solvable but assigned to the wrong asset; conversely, a missing converter
terminal is invalid regardless of its statistical frequency. The two forms of
assessment are therefore complementary rather than interchangeable.

This positioning is also consistent with recent anomaly-detection research.
Physics-informed detection combines learned patterns with physical residuals
to identify abnormal measurements or equipment behavior
~\cite{wu2024piml,guato2024anomaly}. Such methods require measurements,
historical samples, or a reference distribution. A missing terminal, duplicate
identity, or incompatible converter role is instead a defect in model
formation and can be identified before measurements are available. Conversely,
a plausible but incorrect load value may pass the proposed structural
assessment and must be examined using measurement-based methods. The numerical
study therefore evaluates both classes and reports their boundary explicitly.

This paper develops semantics-preserving projection theory (SPPT) as a
device-to-equation and equation-to-device consistency framework for hybrid
AC/DC distribution analysis. Its main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.7em,itemsep=2pt]
\item An analysis-indexed family of projections from detailed engineering
models to canonical hybrid AC/DC representations. Representation invariance,
transformation composition, and a reverse relation classify calculated
quantities as exactly recoverable, bounded approximations,
conservation-based allocations, or unsupported quantities.
\item A converter-role formulation that distinguishes specified variables,
balancing variables, AC angle references, and DC-voltage-regulating devices.
The resulting island conditions are evaluated before the nonlinear calculation.
\item An AI-assisted modification procedure in which the proposed change is
first evaluated on an isolated copy of the engineering model. Only modifications
that satisfy the physical, topological, and device-association conditions are
allowed to replace the accepted model.
\item A distribution-focused evaluation comprising seven public GridLAB-D
feeders with disclosed AC/DC additions, 1575 fixed-seed variants, six structural
fault classes, two plausible parameter-error classes, layer-removal studies,
three-phase stress tests, external-engine comparisons, and 42 AI-assisted
modification cases.
\end{enumerate}
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~2 defines the
hybrid AC/DC equations and the required consistency conditions. Section~3
develops the projection and device-level reconstruction. Section~4 introduces
converter-role closure and the constrained AI modification procedure.
Sections~5 and 6 present the evaluation design and numerical results,
respectively. Section~7 discusses the scope and limitations, and Section~8
draws the conclusions.

\section{Formulation of the modeling consistency problem}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Hybrid AC/DC nodal equations}

Let $S$ contain AC and DC buses, lines, transformers, switches, loads,
distributed resources, storage, VSCs, DC/DC converters, units, phase labels,
controls, and stable device identifiers. The canonical model $C=\Proj(S)$
retains the nodal variables and terminal-equivalent components required by a
declared analysis. Its balanced AC equations are
\begin{align}
P_i^{\rm sp}-\Re\{V_i\sum_jY_{ij}^{*}V_j^{*}\}&=0,\\
Q_i^{\rm sp}-\Im\{V_i\sum_jY_{ij}^{*}V_j^{*}\}&=0,
\label{eq:acbalance}
\end{align}
and the DC equation is
\begin{equation}
P_k^{\rm sp}-v_k\sum_{\ell}G_{k\ell}v_{\ell}=0.
\label{eq:dcbalance}
\end{equation}
A VSC couples the domains through selected control equations and
\begin{equation}
P_{{\rm ac},c}+P_{{\rm dc},c}+P_{{\rm loss},c}=0,
\qquad P_{{\rm loss},c}\ge0.
\label{eq:vscbalance}
\end{equation}
The converter loss can be represented by a constant efficiency or a current-
dependent characteristic. For a converter transferring power from the AC to
the DC side under the constant-efficiency representation,
\begin{equation}
 P_{{\rm dc},c}=-\eta_c P_{{\rm ac},c}, \qquad
 0<\eta_c\le 1,
 \label{eq:efficiency}
\end{equation}
with the signs reversed consistently for the opposite direction. Equation
\eqref{eq:vscbalance} is retained explicitly because an incorrect sign or an
efficiency greater than unity can otherwise introduce artificial generation.

Let $\bm{x}_{\rm ac}=[\bm{\theta},\bm{|V|},\bm{P}_{\rm bal},
\bm{Q}_{\rm bal}]$ and $\bm{x}_{\rm dc}=[\bm{v},\bm{P}_{\rm dc,bal}]$ denote
the AC and DC unknowns after the controlled quantities have been removed. The
converter control equations are collected in $\bm{h}_c$. The complete nodal
problem is written as
\begin{equation}
 \bm{F}(\bm{x};S)=
 \begin{bmatrix}
 \bm{F}_{\rm ac}(\bm{x}_{\rm ac})\\
 \bm{F}_{\rm dc}(\bm{x}_{\rm dc})\\
 \bm{F}_{\rm c}(\bm{x}_{\rm ac},\bm{x}_{\rm dc})\\
 \bm{h}_{\rm c}(\bm{x})
 \end{bmatrix}=\bm{0},
 \label{eq:complete-residual}
\end{equation}
where $S$ denotes the engineering model from which the equation set is formed.
The dimension and physical interpretation of $\bm{x}$ depend on the converter
roles. A $P$--$Q$ device specifies active and reactive exchange and leaves its
DC-side transfer to the coupling equation. A $V_{dc}$--$Q$ device specifies DC
voltage and reactive power and therefore supplies the balancing active power.
An AC grid-forming converter specifies voltage magnitude and angle, whereas a
droop-controlled device introduces a relation between voltage and power rather
than a rigid reference.

The distinction between specified and balancing quantities is important. Two
devices with the same electrical terminals but different controls do not
produce the same equation set. Similarly, assigning the numerical value
$v=1.0$~p.u. as an initial condition does not create a physical DC-voltage
reference. The model must contain a device whose control law supplies that
role. These observations motivate a consistency assessment that is performed
before evaluating \eqref{eq:complete-residual}.

\subsection{Engineering and canonical representations}

The engineering model $S$ contains devices rather than only nodal injections.
For each component $d\in\mathcal D_S$, it records a stable identity, electrical
domain, terminals, phase connection, units, operating status, control mode, and
applicable limits. Transformers, multi-terminal converters, switches, and
energy routers can therefore contain more information than is required by a
particular steady-state calculation. The canonical representation
$C=\Proj(S)$ contains the buses, branches, injections, and control equations
needed to evaluate \eqref{eq:complete-residual}.

The reduction in description is useful but creates two obligations. First,
every canonical component must originate from one or more engineering devices.
Second, every requested calculated quantity must have a defined relation to the
engineering model. These obligations are expressed using a component relation
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma_S\subseteq \mathcal D_S\times\mathcal D_C,
 \label{eq:component-relation}
\end{equation}
where $(d_s,d_c)\in\Gamma_S$ means that canonical component $d_c$ contains a
declared contribution from engineering device $d_s$. The relation is generally
many-to-many: one converter may expand into several terminal components, and
several buses may be merged across an ideal closed switch. The associated
weights must satisfy conservation for extensive quantities such as active
power, whereas intensive quantities such as voltage are broadcast only within
an electrically merged class.

Let $A_C$ denote an analysis performed on $C$ and let $\Rmap_S$ denote the
reverse device association induced by $\Gamma_S$. A result is meaningful only
if its identity space, units, electrical domain, and approximation level are
known. Reporting ``bus 17 voltage'' without stating whether 17 is an AC bus, a
DC bus, an internal nodal position, or an engineering identifier is therefore
not accepted. This requirement is independent of the numerical method used to
solve \eqref{eq:complete-residual}.

\subsection{Why convergence is not a trust criterion}

A nonlinear solution establishes that the residual of the assembled equation
set is small. It does not establish that every authored terminal exists, that
an efficiency is physical, that a DC voltage is controlled by a real device, or
that a canonical injection can be associated with an asset. For example, an
unresolved converter can be omitted during conversion, after which the reduced
AC system may still converge. The numerical result is valid for the altered
equation set but not for the authored hybrid network. \Cref{tab:trust-layers}
separates the required forms of evidence.

For requested observables $\mathcal O$, the model is accepted when
\begin{equation}
\Accept(S,\mathcal O)=\Valid(S)\wedge\WP(\Proj(S))
\wedge\Attrib(\Rmap_S,\mathcal O).
\label{eq:model-acceptance}
\end{equation}
The three terms assess engineering-model integrity, structural closure of the
island roles, and complete device association for the requested outputs. The
numerical solution and independently formed residual are evaluated only after
these conditions have been satisfied. Operating intent remains an external
requirement because it cannot be inferred from one internally consistent model.

Equation~\eqref{eq:model-acceptance} is a necessary condition for using the model in
the declared analysis, not a universal proof of system security. It does not
guarantee that the operating point is sufficiently far from voltage collapse,
that converter current limits remain inactive, or that uncertain parameters
match field measurements. These properties require continuation analysis,
limit handling, estimation, or uncertainty quantification in addition to the
model-consistency assessment developed here.

\section{Semantics-preserving projection and device association}\label{sec:projection}

\Cref{fig:framework} presents the overall concept. Heterogeneous engineering
data and AI-proposed modifications first define a detailed device model. The
projection then forms the nodal equations while retaining the correspondence
between each transformation and the affected physical devices. Calculated
voltages and powers are obtained only from the physical equations; AI-generated
numerical values cannot replace the power-flow solution.

\subsection{Sequence of physical transformations}

The projection is
\begin{equation}
\Proj=\mathcal D\circ\mathcal M\circ\mathcal E\circ\mathcal U,
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal U$ normalizes units and bases, $\mathcal E$ expands compound
devices into terminal equivalents, $\mathcal M$ treats declared ideal
connections, and $\mathcal D$ separates de-energized islands. The order is
important. A device must first be expressed on a common electrical base before
its terminals can be compared; expansion must precede topology treatment so
that an internal transformer or converter terminal is not merged as if it were
an external bus.

Unit normalization $\mathcal U$ converts dimensional quantities to a declared
system base while preserving the authored value and unit. For a branch with
dimensional impedance $Z_{ij}$ at voltage base $V_b$ and power base $S_b$,
\begin{equation}
 z_{ij}^{\rm pu}=Z_{ij}\frac{S_b}{V_b^2}.
 \label{eq:unit-normalization}
\end{equation}
The AC and DC domains use their own voltage bases. A common numerical bus index
is therefore insufficient to identify a voltage base; the domain and nominal
voltage must accompany the identity.

Device expansion $\mathcal E$ replaces a compound device by the smallest set of
terminal components required by the selected analysis. A two-terminal VSC, for
example, contributes an AC terminal, a DC terminal, the coupling balance in
\eqref{eq:vscbalance}, and the equations associated with its control mode. The
engineering identity remains common to these canonical components through
$\Gamma_S$. A transformer or energy router is treated similarly. Expansion is
exact for the variables represented by the selected steady-state model; omitted
dynamic, harmonic, protection, or manufacturer-control quantities are reported
outside the analysis scope.

Topology treatment $\mathcal M$ contracts only connections declared ideal for
the selected analysis. Let $\sim_0$ denote the equivalence relation generated by
closed switches and branches whose impedance is exactly zero. The canonical bus
associated with bus $i$ is the equivalence class
\begin{equation}
 [i]_0=\{j\,|\,j\sim_0 i\}.
 \label{eq:merge-class}
\end{equation}
Contracting a small but nonzero impedance is an approximation rather than an
identity. It is allowed only when the threshold, affected branches, and
observable-dependent error limit are stated. This distinction prevents a
convenient numerical tolerance from being presented as an exact physical
connection.

Finally, $\mathcal D$ identifies connected components after the switching state
and terminal expansion have been applied. De-energized components are retained
in the engineering description but excluded from an energized power-flow
equation set. Their voltages and flows are reported as unavailable rather than
set silently to zero. Energized AC and DC islands proceed to the control-role
assessment described in Section~\ref{sec:ai-modification}.

\subsection{Observable-dependent preservation}

Preservation is defined for an observable, not for the model as an undivided
object. Let $\mathcal O$ be the set of requested quantities and partition it as
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal O=\mathcal O_{\rm ex}\cup\mathcal O_{\rm ap}
 \cup\mathcal O_{\rm co}\cup\mathcal O_{\rm un},
 \label{eq:observable-partition}
\end{equation}
where the four subsets denote exact, bounded-approximate,
conservation-associated, and unsupported quantities, respectively. The subsets
are disjoint for a declared analysis and projection setting.

For $o\in\mathcal O_{\rm ex}$, the engineering and canonical evaluations agree
after reverse association:
\begin{equation}
\Rmap_S\!\left(A_C(\Proj(S));o\right)=A_S(S;o).
\label{eq:commuting}
\end{equation}
Examples include voltage magnitude at buses joined by an ideal connection and
the total active power of an exactly expanded converter. Exactness does not mean
that every internal device variable is available; it applies only to the
declared observable.

For $o\in\mathcal O_{\rm ap}$, equality is replaced by
\begin{equation}
\left\|\Rmap_S\!\left(A_C(\Proj(S));o\right)-A_S(S;o)\right\|
\le\epsilon_{\Pi,o}.
\label{eq:approximate-contract}
\end{equation}
The tolerance $\epsilon_{\Pi,o}$ is attached to the physical quantity and unit.
A voltage error bound cannot be reused as a branch-power bound. Approximate
contraction is therefore accepted only for observables for which a meaningful
bound can be stated.

For $o\in\mathcal O_{\rm co}$, several engineering devices contribute to one
canonical result and no unique physical split exists. In this case the method
asserts origin and conservation,
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{d\in\mathcal D_o} \widehat{o}_d=o_C,
 \qquad (d,d_C)\in\Gamma_S,
 \label{eq:conservation-association}
\end{equation}
but does not claim that a participation-based allocation is a measured device
value. Quantities in $\mathcal O_{\rm un}$ are returned as unsupported. This is
preferable to assigning a plausible value without a physical reconstruction
rule.

\subsection{Composition and device-level reconstruction}

The correspondence record is updated after every transformation. Unit changes
store the original and normalized quantities; expansion records the parent
device of every terminal component; topology treatment records each bus class
and branch origin; island separation records whether an object participates in
the energized equation set. The combined relation provides the reverse map
$\Rmap_S$ used in \eqref{eq:commuting}--\eqref{eq:conservation-association}.

Two reconstruction rules are required. Intensive quantities are broadcast only
when the transformation imposes equality. If buses $i$ and $j$ belong to the
same ideal merge class, their reconstructed voltage is the canonical class
voltage. Extensive quantities are allocated according to declared physical
origins or participation factors whose sum is unity. Mixing these rules would,
for example, duplicate total power when a canonical injection is returned to
several original devices.

The reverse relation also supports diagnosis. When a canonical equation cannot
be formed, the responsible engineering device and field are obtained through
$\Gamma_S$. When a requested result has no reverse relation, the quantity is
rejected before reporting. Thus device association is evaluated both in the
forward direction, where devices must reach the intended equations, and in the
reverse direction, where calculated quantities must return to engineering
identities.

Three quantitative indices are used in the numerical assessment. The device
association coverage for a requested output set is
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_{\mathcal O}=
 \frac{|\mathcal O_{\rm ex}|+|\mathcal O_{\rm ap}|+|\mathcal O_{\rm co}|}
 {|\mathcal O|}.
 \label{eq:association-coverage}
\end{equation}
A result set is reported as fully associated only when
$\kappa_{\mathcal O}=1$; unsupported quantities remain visible in the
denominator rather than being removed. For a conservation-associated
quantity, the normalized reconstruction discrepancy is
\begin{equation}
 \delta_o=\frac{|\sum_{d\in\mathcal D_o}\widehat{o}_d-o_C|}
 {\max(|o_C|,o_{\rm base})},
 \label{eq:reconstruction-error}
\end{equation}
where $o_{\rm base}$ prevents an ill-conditioned relative error near zero. The
declared conservation tolerance is applied to $\delta_o$, while an approximate
observable is assessed against its quantity-specific bound in
\eqref{eq:approximate-contract}.

Finally, the ordering of component records must not change either the calculated
quantity or its engineering identity. Let $\sigma(S)$ denote a permutation of
component records that leaves all identities and physical data unchanged. The required
invariance is
\begin{equation}
 \Rmap_{\sigma(S)}A_C\Proj(\sigma(S))
 =\Rmap_S A_C\Proj(S)
 \quad\text{on }\mathcal O_{\rm ex}.
 \label{eq:permutation-invariance}
\end{equation}
This condition is stronger than comparing two unlabeled voltage vectors: it
requires equality after both vectors have been returned to the same stable
engineering identities. The 175 valid permutations in the numerical campaign
evaluate this requirement across all seven public feeders.

\subsection{Generality across devices, data representations, and analyses}
\label{sec:generality}

SPPT is an analysis-indexed family of physical projections rather than a fixed
catalogue of model checks. Let
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak A=(\mathcal X_{\mathfrak A},\mathcal F_{\mathfrak A},
 \mathcal O_{\mathfrak A},\mathcal E_{\mathfrak A})
 \label{eq:analysis-signature}
\end{equation}
denote an analysis specification containing its variables, governing
equations, requested observables, and declared approximations. The corresponding
projection $\Proj_{\mathfrak A}:S\mapsto C_{\mathfrak A}$ retains the part of an
engineering model required by this specification. A balanced power flow, a
three-phase power flow, a state estimator, and a dynamic study therefore need
not share one canonical model; they share the requirement that the projection
preserve the physical meaning of their declared observables.

This formulation gives a representation-invariance condition. Let
$S^{(1)}\simeq_{\mathcal O}S^{(2)}$ mean that two descriptions encode the same
physical devices, connections, parameters, controls, and operating state for
the observables $\mathcal O$, after declared unit and naming differences have
been resolved. Then an exact observable must satisfy
\begin{equation}
 S^{(1)}\simeq_{\mathcal O}S^{(2)}\Longrightarrow
 \Rmap_{S^{(1)}}A_{C^{(1)}}\!\left(\Proj_{\mathfrak A}(S^{(1)})\right)
 =
 \Rmap_{S^{(2)}}A_{C^{(2)}}\!\left(\Proj_{\mathfrak A}(S^{(2)})\right).
 \label{eq:representation-invariance}
\end{equation}
For bounded-approximate observables, equality is replaced by the declared bound
in \eqref{eq:approximate-contract}. Mature environments such as GridLAB-D,
OpenDSS, MATPOWER, pandapower, and PowerModelsDistribution illustrate the
diversity of power-system descriptions and analysis formulations
~\cite{gridlabd,opendss,pmd,thurner2018pandapower,zimmerman2011matpower}.
Equation~\eqref{eq:representation-invariance} does not require these
environments to use identical internal variables; it requires physically
equivalent inputs to agree after results have been returned to common
engineering quantities.

The same principle applies to a sequence of transformations. If $T_1$ and
$T_2$ each retain a declared observable and their device relations are
$\Gamma_{T_1}$ and $\Gamma_{T_2}$, then the composed transformation uses
\begin{equation}
 \Gamma_{T_2\circ T_1}=\Gamma_{T_1}\circ\Gamma_{T_2},
 \label{eq:relation-composition}
\end{equation}
and must satisfy the same conservation, topology, and reconstruction conditions
as a direct transformation. This compositional requirement prevents an
individually reasonable unit conversion, device expansion, or topology
reduction from losing physical meaning when the operations are combined.

\Cref{tab:generality} states what must be supplied when the scope is extended.
A new device is not accommodated by adding a device-name rule. It is described
by its terminals, balance contribution, active control role, and reconstruction
of requested quantities. Likewise, a new analysis declares the variables and
equations it requires, while a new aggregation declares the observables and
conservation relations it preserves. The acceptance conditions in
\eqref{eq:model-acceptance} can then be derived from these declarations. The
six fault classes used later test selected violations of the general
conditions; they do not define SPPT itself.

\section{Converter-role closure and constrained AI modification}\label{sec:ai-modification}

\subsection{Converter control roles}

Converter modes determine which equations constrain the system and which
variables power balance must recover. A $P$--$Q$ converter is grid-following
and contributes no voltage reference; a $V_{dc}$--$Q$ or DC-droop converter
anchors DC potential; and an AC grid-forming role supplies the island angle
reference. The assessment uses these physical roles rather than device-class
names because identical converter hardware can operate under different controls.

For converter $c$, define the role description
\begin{equation}
 \rho_c=(\mathcal Y_c,\mathcal X_c,\mathcal R_c),
 \label{eq:role-tuple}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal Y_c$ contains regulated quantities, $\mathcal X_c$ contains
quantities recovered from power balance, and $\mathcal R_c$ contains the AC or
DC reference contribution. For example,
\begin{align}
 \rho_c^{P-Q}&=(\{P_{ac},Q_{ac}\},\{P_{dc}\},\varnothing),\\
 \rho_c^{V_{dc}-Q}&=(\{v_{dc},Q_{ac}\},\{P_{ac},P_{dc}\},\{v_{dc}\}).
 \label{eq:role-examples}
\end{align}
The sets are interpreted together with \eqref{eq:vscbalance}; specifying both
AC and DC active powers independently would generally overconstrain the loss
balance, whereas specifying neither side leaves the balancing exchange
unassigned.

\Cref{tab:converter-roles} summarizes the role assignment used in the
assessment. The table separates voltage-magnitude regulation from an angle or
frequency reference because the former does not remove the rotational degree
of freedom of an AC island. It also separates a fixed DC-voltage setpoint from
DC droop. Both provide voltage support, but the droop-controlled devices share
the balancing duty through their slopes rather than imposing several identical
voltage equations. The assignment is made from the active control mode and
connection status, not from the hardware nameplate.

The role description is evaluated after the switching state and energized
islands have been determined. A converter disconnected on either side does not
contribute a reference to that island. Similarly, a voltage limit, initial
value, or nominal rating does not contribute to $\mathcal R_c$ unless it is part
of an active physical control law.

\subsection{Island-level structural closure}

For every energized AC island $\mathcal I_a$ and DC island $\mathcal I_d$,
\begin{equation}
n_{\theta}(\mathcal I_a)=1,\qquad n_v(\mathcal I_d)\ge1,
\label{eq:rolecondition}
\end{equation}
where $n_{\theta}$ counts compatible angle-forming roles and $n_v$ counts
physical DC voltage or droop support. Multiple rigid references require an
explicit coordination law. These conditions are necessary role-closure tests:
they do not guarantee Jacobian nonsingularity away from the operating point,
distance from voltage collapse, or satisfaction of converter limits.

The role counts are supplemented by an equation--variable incidence test. Let
$\mathcal F_{\mathcal I}$ and $\mathcal X_{\mathcal I}$ denote the equations and
unknowns associated with an energized island. The structural incidence matrix
is
\begin{equation}
 B_{pq}=\begin{cases}
 1,&\partial F_p/\partial x_q\not\equiv0,\\
 0,&\text{otherwise},
 \end{cases}
 \quad F_p\in\mathcal F_{\mathcal I},\ x_q\in\mathcal X_{\mathcal I}.
 \label{eq:structural-incidence}
\end{equation}
After the reference variables have been removed, a necessary closure condition
is
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{sprank}(\bm{B}_{\mathcal I})
 =|\mathcal X_{\mathcal I}|.
 \label{eq:structural-rank}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{sprank}$ is the maximum matching size of the bipartite graph
between equations and variables. This test identifies a missing control
equation without relying on a particular initial point. It is followed, rather
than replaced, by a numerical Jacobian check at the calculated operating point.

An island can fail in three distinct ways. With no compatible reference, at
least one voltage or angle variable remains unmatched. With multiple rigid
references and no coordination law, the equation set can be overconstrained.
With a formally complete role assignment, the numerical Jacobian can still
become ill-conditioned near voltage collapse or a converter limit. Separating
these cases improves interpretation: the first two are model-formation errors,
whereas the third is an operating-condition property.

\subsection{Constrained AI-assisted model modification}

The role of AI is limited to interpreting an engineering request and proposing a
model modification. It does not generate an accepted voltage, current, or power
result. Each proposal is represented by
\begin{equation}
 m=(\text{operation},\text{asset},\text{quantity},\text{value},
 \text{permitted use},\text{evidence}),
 \label{eq:structured-modification}
\end{equation}
where the asset must resolve to a stable engineering identity and the quantity
must be applicable to that device type. The permitted use distinguishes a
read-only analysis request, an engineering-model modification, an advisory
operating proposal, and a direct physical control request. The present study
evaluates only the first two categories.

Let $S_k$ be the most recent accepted model. The proposed model is formed as
$S'_k=m_k(S_k)$ without altering $S_k$. Define $\chi(S'_k,\mathcal O)=1$ when
the model-integrity, role-closure, device-association, and numerical conditions
for observables $\mathcal O$ have been satisfied. The accepted sequence is
\begin{equation}
 S_{k+1}=\begin{cases}
 S'_k,&\chi(S'_k,\mathcal O)=1,\\
 S_k,&\chi(S'_k,\mathcal O)=0.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:model-update}
\end{equation}
Consequently, an inadmissible proposal cannot alter the model used by the next
analysis. The associated diagnosis identifies the physical asset, field, or
island condition responsible for refusal. This property is assessed explicitly
in the numerical study by applying a valid modification after every rejected
proposal.

Equation~\eqref{eq:model-update} does not authorize autonomous physical
control. A model modification can be internally consistent while still being
based on an incorrect operating instruction. Direct control therefore requires
additional authorization, telemetry consistency, operating limits, and human
oversight outside the scope of the present work.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why convergence is insufficient}

The results change the interpretation of model verification in
converter-dominated distribution systems. Convergence-based screening detects
the missing AC angle reference because this error directly affects the numerical
degrees of freedom. It does not detect missing converter terminals, missing DC
support, or an efficiency above unity in the tested cases. These errors either
remove a component from the assembled equations or leave a smooth but
physically incorrect equation set. A small residual consequently demonstrates
consistency between a numerical solution and the equations that were actually
formed; it does not demonstrate consistency between those equations and the
engineering system that was intended.

This distinction becomes more important as the share of power-electronic
equipment increases. In a passive AC feeder, a missing branch commonly changes
connectivity and may cause an obvious island. In a hybrid AC/DC network, a
converter can be connected topologically while its control role is inconsistent
with the balancing requirements. The network graph alone is therefore
insufficient. Model-integrity checks, converter-role closure, and numerical
solution address different failure mechanisms and must be applied in that
order.

The layer-removal results support this interpretation quantitatively. Removing
the role assessment produces a complete blind spot for 175 missing-DC-support
cases. Removing the engineering checks and retaining convergence creates four
complete blind spots and detects only 3 of 75 duplicate-identity cases. The
independent residual does not change the structural-fault count because those
models are refused before calculation, but it provides a separate check against
errors in equation formation for accepted models. No single layer reproduces
the complete result.

\subsection{Value of device-level association}

Device association has practical value beyond reproducibility. A generic
message that a nodal equation cannot be formed does not identify the converter,
terminal, or control quantity that must be corrected. The forward relation
$\Gamma_S$ locates the engineering origin of the failed equation, while the
reverse relation $\Rmap_S$ associates calculated voltages and powers with stable
asset identities. This is required for any subsequent planning or operating
decision because a canonical bus position is not itself an engineering asset.

The distinction between exact, approximate, conservation-associated, and
unsupported quantities also prevents overinterpretation of reduced models.
Exact bus-voltage reconstruction across an ideal merge class is fundamentally
different from allocating an aggregate power among several devices. The latter
may satisfy conservation without representing a measured device value. Stating
this difference is particularly important when AI is used to summarize results,
because fluent language can otherwise hide the physical nonuniqueness of an
allocation.

\subsection{Relation to anomaly detection and state estimation}

SPPT and anomaly detection address different uncertainty classes. SPPT refuses
models that are internally invalid, structurally incomplete, or incapable of
returning a requested result to an engineering device. It intentionally accepts
a physically plausible load or converter-setpoint change when the structural
conditions remain satisfied. Whether that change is true cannot be determined
from the model alone.

The 350 plausible parameter modifications demonstrate this boundary. Every
modification passes the structural assessment, yet the maximum AC-voltage and
branch-power deviations reach $4.239\times10^{-3}$~p.u. and 0.526~MW,
respectively. These values are not structural failures; they are the physical
consequences of uncertain operating data. Telemetry comparison, state and
parameter estimation, historical anomaly detection, and operator confirmation
are appropriate for this second layer. The proposed method should therefore be
combined with, rather than presented as a replacement for, measurement-based
assessment.

\subsection{Implications for AI-assisted energy analysis}

The AI contribution in this study is deliberately limited. AI interprets a
request and proposes a structured engineering modification, while the accepted
electrical quantities remain outputs of the physical model. This division avoids
using language-model confidence as a substitute for power-system evidence. The
42 public-feeder modification combinations show that admissible changes can be
adopted and inadmissible changes can be refused without altering the previously
accepted model. The subsequent valid assessment in every case provides direct
evidence that refusal does not damage the model used for later analysis.

These results should not be interpreted as a statistical evaluation of general
language-model reliability. The optional five-request local trial only
demonstrates that a language request can be expressed using the constrained
modification variables in \eqref{eq:structured-modification}. Reliability under
arbitrary prompts, model versions, languages, or adversarial instructions would
require a substantially larger and separately designed study. The contribution
here is the physical assessment that remains applicable regardless of which AI
method proposes the modification.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

The primary campaign uses a balanced steady-state subset because the public
GridLAB-D conversion does not retain every unbalanced control and protection
quantity. The staged three-phase cases extend the assessment to balanced,
unbalanced radial, and unbalanced meshed conditions, but they do not establish
equivalence between the positive-sequence and phase-domain models. Likewise,
the GridLAB-D and OpenDSS comparisons are limited to physical quantities shared
by the participating models. Neither external engine is treated as a reference
for the added DC network and converter controls.

The island role counts and structural incidence test are necessary formation
conditions, not sufficient operating-security conditions. They do not exclude
voltage collapse, converter saturation, protection operation, discrete control
changes, or dynamic instability. These phenomena require continuation power
flow, complementarity or active-set treatment, dynamic simulation, and
protection models. The framework can carry the corresponding device association
into those analyses, but the present numerical evidence is centered on
steady-state model formation.

Finally, perfect detection in the six injected structural classes is not a
universal completeness result. Correlated parameter errors, time-varying data
drift, malicious measurement changes, and undocumented manufacturer controls
remain outside the tested population. The fixed campaign and disclosed public
systems provide a reproducible comparison, while broader field validation will
be required before operational use.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper developed a verification framework for AI-assisted modeling of
converter-dominated hybrid AC/DC distribution systems. The framework projects
detailed engineering devices to a canonical nodal representation, retains the
forward and reverse device correspondence, classifies converter control roles,
and evaluates island-level structural closure before nonlinear calculation.
AI-proposed modifications are assessed on an isolated model and replace the
accepted model only when the declared physical and numerical conditions are
satisfied. Its generality follows from representation invariance, compositional
transformations, observable-dependent reconstruction, and analysis-specific
equation--variable closure rather than from an enumeration of fault rules.

The evaluation contains 1575 fixed-seed variants of seven public distribution
feeders with 32--6986 AC buses and fully disclosed AC/DC additions. The complete
method detects and localizes all 1050 structural faults and refuses none of the
175 admissible permutations. Convergence-based screening misses all tested
missing-terminal, missing-DC-support, and invalid-efficiency cases and detects
only 3 of 75 duplicate-identity cases. The constrained AI experiment correctly
accepts 14 admissible modifications, refuses 28 inadmissible modifications, and
retains a usable accepted model after every refusal. Three-phase stress studies
and GridLAB-D/OpenDSS comparisons further define the range of the evaluation.

The parameter-error study establishes an equally important limitation:
internally consistent but incorrect loads and converter setpoints remain
admissible and can produce material voltage and power deviations. Trustworthy
AI-assisted analysis therefore requires two complementary forms of evidence.
The proposed framework establishes physical model consistency and device-level
association, while telemetry, estimation, and operator review establish whether
the numerical data represent the intended operating system. Future work will
extend this combination to broader phase-domain conversion, converter limits,
dynamic models, and uncertainty-aware operating decisions.
