% ============================================================
\section{EV Power--Traffic Dynamic Simulation: Theoretical Model}
\label{sec:ev_power_traffic_dynamic_model}
% ============================================================

This chapter defines a theory-first model for extending the current
\texttt{HybridPowerSystem} infrastructure to a coupled electric-vehicle
power--traffic dynamic simulator.  It is intentionally a mathematical contract,
not an implementation claim.  The existing code already contains AC/DC network
models, time-series power-flow assembly, \texttt{ChargingStation}, and
\texttt{Charger} component tables.  The traffic graph, time-varying OD demand,
dynamic traffic assignment, charging-session state, queueing state, and
station-level smart charging controller are extension points to be designed
after the model below is accepted.

The model has four coupled layers:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item time-varying EV travel demand and dynamic traffic assignment,
  \item station-choice and charging-session generation,
  \item smart charging or V2G dispatch at charging stations,
  \item time-series AC/DC power-flow or OPF feasibility.
\end{enumerate}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Compatibility With the Current Data Model}
\label{sec:evpt-compatibility}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The current infrastructure can already represent the electrical side of each
charging site:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline\hline
\textbf{Concept} & \textbf{Current field} & \textbf{Role in this model} \\
\hline
Station-bus mapping & \texttt{ChargingStation::bus}
  & maps EV power to an AC bus \\
Station capacity & \texttt{max\_power\_kw}, \texttt{n\_fast}, \texttt{n\_slow}
  & aggregate charging-power limits \\
Station demand & \texttt{p\_total\_kw}, \texttt{q\_total\_kvar}
  & time-indexed EV load after dispatch \\
Power factor & \texttt{power\_factor}
  & reactive-load approximation \\
Charger link & \texttt{Charger::station\_id}
  & maps individual chargers to a station \\
Charger limits & \texttt{p\_ch\_max\_kw}, \texttt{p\_ch\_min\_kw}
  & per-plug power bounds \\
Efficiency & \texttt{Charger::eta}
  & battery-energy conversion \\
V2G capability & \texttt{v2g\_capable}, \texttt{p\_dis\_max\_kw}
  & reverse-power bounds \\
\hline\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

New model objects are still required for the transportation layer:
\texttt{TrafficNode}, \texttt{TrafficLink}, time-dependent OD demand,
EV trip classes, route/charging alternatives, station queue state, charging
sessions, and a coupled simulation driver.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Time Grid, Sets, and Units}
\label{sec:evpt-sets}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Let the simulation horizon be discretized as
\[
  \mathcal{T}=\{0,1,\ldots,N_T\}, \qquad
  t_k = k\Delta t,
\]
where \(\Delta t\) is measured in hours unless stated otherwise.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\hline\hline
\textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\(\mathcal{G}^{tr}=(\mathcal{N}^{tr},\mathcal{A}^{tr})\)
  & directed traffic graph \\
\(\mathcal{W}\) & OD pairs \(w=(o,d)\) \\
\(\mathcal{C}\) & EV classes, e.g. private, taxi, bus, fleet \\
\(\mathcal{R}_{w,c}\) & route/charging alternatives for OD pair \(w\), class \(c\) \\
\(\mathcal{S}\) & charging stations \\
\(b(s)\in\mathcal{B}^{ac}\) & AC bus serving station \(s\) \\
\(\mathcal{B}^{ac},\mathcal{L}^{ac}\) & AC buses and AC branches \\
\(\mathcal{B}^{dc},\mathcal{L}^{dc}\) & DC buses and DC branches, if present \\
\(\mathcal{I}_s\) & charging sessions assigned to station \(s\) \\
\hline\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Traffic demand is measured in vehicles per time step or vehicles per hour.
Station power is naturally stored by the current model in kW/kvar, while the
power-flow and OPF solvers use MW/Mvar.  The conversion factor is
\[
  P^{EV,MW}_{s,k}=10^{-3}P^{EV,kW}_{s,k}.
\]

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Time-Varying Travel Demand}
\label{sec:evpt-demand}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

For each OD pair \(w\in\mathcal{W}\), EV class \(c\in\mathcal{C}\), and
departure interval \(k\in\mathcal{T}\), let
\[
  d_{w,c,k}\ge 0
\]
be the exogenous time-varying EV departure demand.  If elastic demand is later
required, the fixed profile can be replaced by
\[
  d_{w,c,k}
  =
  \bar d_{w,c,k}\,
  \exp\!\left[-\gamma_{w,c}
  \left(\Pi_{w,c,k}-\bar \Pi_{w,c,k}\right)\right],
  \label{eq:evpt-elastic-demand}
\]
where \(\Pi_{w,c,k}\) is the experienced generalized travel-and-charging cost.
For the first implementation target, \(d_{w,c,k}\) should be treated as an
input time series, so that the traffic and power coupling can be validated
without demand-elasticity feedback.

Let \(h_{w,c,r,k}\ge 0\) denote the departure flow assigned to
route/charging alternative \(r\in\mathcal{R}_{w,c}\).  Conservation of
departures gives
\begin{align}
  \sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{w,c}} h_{w,c,r,k}
  &= d_{w,c,k},
  &&\forall w,c,k. \label{eq:evpt-path-demand-conservation}
\end{align}

Each alternative \(r\) contains a road path, optional charging stations,
planned charging amounts, and admissible arrival/departure windows.  This keeps
route choice and station choice in one assignment variable.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Dynamic Traffic Flow Model}
\label{sec:evpt-dta-flow}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

For road link \(a\in\mathcal{A}^{tr}\), let \(n_{a,c,k}\) be the number of
class-\(c\) vehicles on the link at time \(k\), \(u_{a,c,k}\) its inflow, and
 \(v_{a,c,k}\) its outflow.  Link conservation is
\begin{align}
  n_{a,c,k+1}
  &=
  n_{a,c,k}
  + \Delta t\left(u_{a,c,k}-v_{a,c,k}\right),
  &&\forall a,c,k. \label{eq:evpt-link-conservation}
\end{align}

Let \(n_{a,k}=\sum_c n_{a,c,k}\).  A link-transmission form can be written as
\begin{align}
  0 \le \sum_c v_{a,c,k}
  &\le S_{a,k}(n_{a,k}), \label{eq:evpt-sending}\\
  0 \le \sum_c u_{a,c,k}
  &\le R_{a,k}(n_{a,k}), \label{eq:evpt-receiving}
\end{align}
with sending and receiving functions, for example,
\begin{align}
  S_{a,k}(n)
  &=
  \min\!\left\{\frac{n}{\Delta t},\,\bar C_{a,k}\right\},\\
  R_{a,k}(n)
  &=
  \min\!\left\{\frac{\bar N^{jam}_{a}-n}{\Delta t},\,\bar C_{a,k}\right\}.
\end{align}
Here \(\bar C_{a,k}\) is the time-dependent road capacity and
\(\bar N^{jam}_{a}\) is the jam accumulation.  The alternative simpler model is
a volume-delay function
\begin{align}
  \tau_{a,k}
  =
  \tau^0_a
  \left[
    1+\alpha_a
    \left(\frac{n_{a,k}}{\bar N_a}\right)^{\beta_a}
  \right],
  \label{eq:evpt-link-delay}
\end{align}
where \(\tau_{a,k}\) is the link traversal time.  The link-transmission form is
preferred for dynamic queues, while the delay-function form is useful for an
initial proof-of-concept.

For a vehicle departing at \(t_k\) on a road sequence
\((a_1,\ldots,a_m)\), arrival times are propagated recursively:
\begin{align}
  \theta_1 &= t_k,\\
  \theta_{\ell+1}
  &=
  \theta_{\ell} + \tau_{a_\ell,\lfloor \theta_\ell/\Delta t\rfloor},
  && \ell=1,\ldots,m. \label{eq:evpt-arrival-recursion}
\end{align}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{EV Battery Feasibility Along Routes}
\label{sec:evpt-route-energy}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Let \(E\) denote battery energy in kWh.  For class \(c\), road link \(a\), and
time \(k\), define per-vehicle traction energy consumption
\[
  e_{a,c,k}^{drive}\ge 0 \quad [\mathrm{kWh/vehicle}].
\]
It may be a constant link coefficient, or a speed-dependent model such as
\[
  e_{a,c,k}^{drive}
  =
  \ell_a\left(
    \alpha_c+\beta_c v_{a,k}^{spd}
    +\chi_c (v_{a,k}^{spd})^2
  \right),
  \label{eq:evpt-drive-energy}
\]
where \(\ell_a\) is link length and \(v_{a,k}^{spd}\) is average speed.

For a route alternative \(r\), the representative vehicle battery evolves as
\begin{align}
  E_{\ell+1}
  &=
  E_{\ell}
  - e_{a_\ell,c,\lfloor \theta_\ell/\Delta t\rfloor}^{drive}
  + e_{\ell}^{chg},
  &&\ell=1,\ldots,m, \label{eq:evpt-route-soc}\\
  \underline E_c
  &\le E_{\ell}\le \overline E_c,
  &&\ell=1,\ldots,m+1. \label{eq:evpt-route-soc-bounds}
\end{align}
The term \(e_{\ell}^{chg}\) is nonzero only when the alternative includes a
charging stop before the next road segment.  A route/charging alternative is
feasible only if \eqref{eq:evpt-route-soc-bounds} holds along the whole trip.

The energy requested at station \(s\) by an assigned alternative is denoted
\[
  e^{req}_{w,c,r,s,k} \quad [\mathrm{kWh/vehicle}].
\]
Station-arrival flow and requested energy are then
\begin{align}
  \lambda_{s,k}
  &=
  \sum_{w,c}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{w,c}}
  A_{s,k}^{w,c,r} h_{w,c,r,k}, \label{eq:evpt-station-arrivals}\\
  E^{arr}_{s,k}
  &=
  \sum_{w,c}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{w,c}}
  A_{s,k}^{w,c,r} e^{req}_{w,c,r,s,k} h_{w,c,r,k},
  \label{eq:evpt-station-energy-arrivals}
\end{align}
where \(A_{s,k}^{w,c,r}\) is a time-shifted incidence coefficient equal to the
number of vehicles from departure interval \(k\) and alternative \(r\) that
reach station \(s\) in the relevant station-arrival interval.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Dynamic Traffic Assignment Equilibrium}
\label{sec:evpt-dta-equilibrium}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Let \(J_{w,c,r,k}\) be the generalized cost of assigning one class-\(c\)
vehicle from OD pair \(w\), departure interval \(k\), to alternative \(r\).
It combines travel time, queueing, charging payment, battery risk, and any
policy charges:
\begin{align}
  J_{w,c,r,k}
  &=
  \omega_T T_{w,c,r,k}
  + \omega_Q \sum_{s\in r} W_{s,\kappa(s,r,k)}
  + \sum_{s\in r} \pi^{chg}_{s,\kappa(s,r,k)}
      e^{req}_{w,c,r,s,k}
  + \omega_R R_{w,c,r,k}
  + \omega_M M_{w,c,r,k}. \label{eq:evpt-generalized-cost}
\end{align}
Here \(T\) is route travel time, \(W_s\) is station waiting time,
\(\pi^{chg}_s\) is the charging price in \$/kWh, \(R\) is a range-risk
penalty, \(M\) represents tolls or mobility policy charges, and
\(\kappa(s,r,k)\) maps departure time to station-arrival time.

A deterministic dynamic user equilibrium (DUE) is
\begin{align}
  h^*_{w,c,r,k} > 0
  &\Rightarrow
  J_{w,c,r,k}(h^*,p^*,P^*) =
  \min_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{w,c}}
  J_{w,c,\rho,k}(h^*,p^*,P^*),
  \label{eq:evpt-due-complementarity}
\end{align}
for every \(w,c,k\).  Equivalently, with feasible set
\[
  \mathcal{H}(d)
  =
  \left\{
  h\ge0:
  \sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{w,c}} h_{w,c,r,k}=d_{w,c,k}
  \ \forall w,c,k
  \right\},
\]
the DUE satisfies the variational inequality
\begin{align}
  \sum_{w,c,k}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{w,c}}
  J_{w,c,r,k}(h^*,p^*,P^*)
  \left(h_{w,c,r,k}-h^*_{w,c,r,k}\right)
  \ge 0,
  \quad \forall h\in\mathcal{H}(d).
  \label{eq:evpt-due-vi}
\end{align}

A stochastic DTA variant can replace \eqref{eq:evpt-due-complementarity} with a
logit assignment:
\begin{align}
  h_{w,c,r,k}
  =
  d_{w,c,k}
  \frac{\exp[-\theta_c J_{w,c,r,k}]}
  {\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}_{w,c}}\exp[-\theta_c J_{w,c,\rho,k}]},
  \label{eq:evpt-logit-dta}
\end{align}
which is often smoother for fixed-point simulation.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Charging Station Queue and Session Model}
\label{sec:evpt-station-session}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

For public fast charging, an aggregate queue model is useful:
\begin{align}
  q_{s,k+1}
  &=
  q_{s,k}+\lambda_{s,k}-u_{s,k},
  \label{eq:evpt-queue-dynamics}\\
  m_{s,k+1}
  &=
  m_{s,k}+u_{s,k}-r_{s,k},
  \label{eq:evpt-occupancy-dynamics}\\
  0\le u_{s,k} &\le q_{s,k}+\lambda_{s,k},\\
  0\le m_{s,k} &\le N^{plug}_s.
\end{align}
Here \(q_{s,k}\) is the waiting queue, \(u_{s,k}\) is the number admitted to
plugs, \(m_{s,k}\) is plug occupancy, \(r_{s,k}\) is completed service, and
\(N^{plug}_s\) is the number of usable plugs.  The waiting time appearing in
\eqref{eq:evpt-generalized-cost} can be computed by a queue discipline or
approximated as
\begin{align}
  W_{s,k}
  \approx
  \frac{q_{s,k}}{\max\{\mu_{s,k},\epsilon\}},
  \label{eq:evpt-wait-approx}
\end{align}
where \(\mu_{s,k}\) is an effective service rate.

For smart charging, the more important representation is a set of charging
sessions.  A session \(i\in\mathcal{I}_s\) is defined by
\[
  i=(s,a_i,d_i,E^0_i,E^{tar}_i,\overline p^{ch}_i,
     \overline p^{dis}_i,\eta^{ch}_i,\eta^{dis}_i,\nu_i),
\]
where \(a_i,d_i\) are arrival and departure intervals, \(E^0_i\) is arrival
battery energy, \(E^{tar}_i\) is the target departure energy,
\(\overline p^{ch}_i\) and \(\overline p^{dis}_i\) are kW limits, and
\(\nu_i\in\{0,1\}\) indicates V2G capability.

Let
\[
  \delta_{i,k}
  =
  \begin{cases}
    1, & a_i\le k<d_i,\\
    0, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
\]
be the plug-in availability of session \(i\).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Station-Level Smart Charging}
\label{sec:evpt-smart-charging}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

For a session \(i\), let \(p^{ch}_{i,k}\ge0\) be charging power, \(p^{dis}_{i,k}
\ge0\) be V2G discharging power, and \(E_{i,k}\) be battery energy.  The
session dynamics are
\begin{align}
  E_{i,k+1}
  &=
  E_{i,k}
  + \Delta t\left(
    \eta^{ch}_i p^{ch}_{i,k}
    - \frac{p^{dis}_{i,k}}{\eta^{dis}_i}
  \right),
  &&\forall i,k, \label{eq:evpt-session-energy}\\
  \underline E_i
  &\le E_{i,k}\le \overline E_i,
  &&\forall i,k, \label{eq:evpt-session-energy-bounds}\\
  E_{i,d_i}+\xi_i
  &\ge E^{tar}_i,
  &&\forall i, \label{eq:evpt-session-deadline}\\
  \xi_i&\ge0,
  &&\forall i. \label{eq:evpt-unserved-energy}
\end{align}

Power bounds are
\begin{align}
  0\le p^{ch}_{i,k}
  &\le \overline p^{ch}_i \delta_{i,k},
  &&\forall i,k, \label{eq:evpt-session-charge-bound}\\
  0\le p^{dis}_{i,k}
  &\le \nu_i\overline p^{dis}_i \delta_{i,k},
  &&\forall i,k. \label{eq:evpt-session-discharge-bound}
\end{align}

Station aggregate net EV power is positive when the station consumes power:
\begin{align}
  P^{EV}_{s,k}
  =
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_s}
  \left(p^{ch}_{i,k}-p^{dis}_{i,k}\right).
  \label{eq:evpt-station-ev-power}
\end{align}
Station capacity constraints are
\begin{align}
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_s} p^{ch}_{i,k}
  &\le \overline P^{ch}_s,\\
  \sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_s} p^{dis}_{i,k}
  &\le \overline P^{dis}_s,
  &&\forall s,k. \label{eq:evpt-station-power-limits}
\end{align}

Let \(\lambda^P_{b(s),k}\) be the active-power locational marginal price or a
network-aware charging signal at the station bus.  A convex station controller
can be written as
\begin{align}
  \min_{p^{ch},p^{dis},E,\xi}
  \quad
  &\sum_{k}
  \left[
    \lambda^P_{b(s),k} P^{EV}_{s,k}\Delta t
    + \frac{r_s}{2}\left(P^{EV}_{s,k}\right)^2\Delta t
    + c^{deg}_s \sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_s}p^{dis}_{i,k}\Delta t
  \right] \notag\\
  &\quad
  + \rho_s\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_s}\xi_i
  + \rho^{ramp}_s\sum_k
  \left(P^{EV}_{s,k}-P^{EV}_{s,k-1}\right)^2
  \label{eq:evpt-station-objective}
\end{align}
subject to
\eqref{eq:evpt-session-energy}--\eqref{eq:evpt-station-power-limits}.
The quadratic term \(r_s\) prevents bang-bang charging when prices are flat,
\(\rho_s\) penalizes missed departure energy, and \(\rho^{ramp}_s\) limits
fast station-level ramps.

\paragraph{Marginal-condition interpretation.}
Ignoring V2G, ramps, and active bounds, the Lagrangian stationarity condition
for a session \(i\) is
\begin{align}
  \lambda^P_{b(s),k}
  + r_s P^{EV}_{s,k}
  - \eta^{ch}_i \mu_i
  = 0,
  \label{eq:evpt-smart-charging-kkt}
\end{align}
where \(\mu_i\) is the shadow value of the departure-energy requirement.
Thus flexible EVs charge when the network price plus station congestion cost
falls below their deadline-adjusted energy value.  With active bounds, the same
condition becomes a projection onto the feasible interval
\([0,\overline p^{ch}_i]\).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Power-System Coupling}
\label{sec:evpt-power-coupling}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

At each time \(k\), EV charging modifies the bus demand.  For an AC bus \(i\),
\begin{align}
  P^{d}_{i,k}
  &=
  P^{base}_{i,k}
  + 10^{-3}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}:b(s)=i} P^{EV}_{s,k},
  \label{eq:evpt-ac-p-load}\\
  Q^{d}_{i,k}
  &=
  Q^{base}_{i,k}
  + 10^{-3}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}:b(s)=i}
  P^{EV}_{s,k}\tan(\arccos \operatorname{pf}_{s}),
  \label{eq:evpt-ac-q-load}
\end{align}
where \(\operatorname{pf}_{s}\) is the station power factor.  If the charger
converter is later modeled with controllable reactive power,
\eqref{eq:evpt-ac-q-load} can be replaced by explicit \(Q^{EV}_{s,k}\)
decision variables and inverter capability curves.

The AC network equations at time \(k\) are
\begin{align}
  P^g_{i,k}-P^d_{i,k}
  &=
  V_{i,k}
  \sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}^{ac}}
  V_{j,k}\left(
    G_{ij}\cos\theta_{ij,k}
    +B_{ij}\sin\theta_{ij,k}
  \right),
  \label{eq:evpt-ac-pf-p}\\
  Q^g_{i,k}-Q^d_{i,k}
  &=
  V_{i,k}
  \sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}^{ac}}
  V_{j,k}\left(
    G_{ij}\sin\theta_{ij,k}
    -B_{ij}\cos\theta_{ij,k}
  \right),
  \label{eq:evpt-ac-pf-q}
\end{align}
with voltage and branch limits
\begin{align}
  \underline V_i \le V_{i,k}\le \overline V_i,
  \qquad
  |S_{\ell,k}|\le \overline S_\ell.
  \label{eq:evpt-ac-operating-limits}
\end{align}

If an OPF layer is used instead of a pure PF feasibility check, the power system
subproblem is
\begin{align}
  \min_{P^g,Q^g,V,\theta,\ldots}
  \quad
  &\sum_{k}\left[
    \sum_{g} C_g(P^g_{g,k})
    + C^{shed}_{k}
    + C^{loss}_{k}
  \right] \label{eq:evpt-opf-objective}\\
  \text{s.t.}\quad
  &\eqref{eq:evpt-ac-p-load}--\eqref{eq:evpt-ac-operating-limits},
    \text{ generator limits, and AC/DC converter limits.}
\end{align}
The dual multiplier of \eqref{eq:evpt-ac-pf-p} is the natural
\(\lambda^P_{i,k}\) signal used by the smart charging model.  If only PF is
available, \(\lambda^P_{i,k}\) may be replaced by an exogenous tariff or by a
network-stress signal derived from voltage and branch-limit margins.

For DC buses and VSC-linked chargers, the same idea applies through the DC
conductance model and converter equations already used elsewhere in the
notebook.  The first EV extension should keep charging stations on AC buses,
because that aligns directly with the current \texttt{ChargingStation::bus}
field and the existing solver-data aggregation path.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Coupled Power--Traffic Dynamic Equilibrium}
\label{sec:evpt-coupled-equilibrium}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The coupled model is a fixed point among four mappings:
\begin{align}
  h
  &\in \operatorname{DTA}
  \left(d,W,\pi^{chg},\text{traffic state}\right),
  \label{eq:evpt-fixed-dta}\\
  \mathcal{I}
  &=
  \Gamma(h,\text{route energy},\text{station arrivals}),
  \label{eq:evpt-fixed-session}\\
  P^{EV}
  &\in
  \operatorname{SmartCharge}
  \left(\mathcal{I},\lambda^P,\text{station limits}\right),
  \label{eq:evpt-fixed-smart-charge}\\
  (V,\theta,P^g,Q^g,\lambda^P)
  &\in
  \operatorname{PowerSolve}
  \left(P^{base}+P^{EV},Q^{base}+Q^{EV}\right).
  \label{eq:evpt-fixed-power}
\end{align}
The outputs \(W\) and \(\pi^{chg}\) in
\eqref{eq:evpt-fixed-dta} must be consistent with the queue and charging
solutions generated by \eqref{eq:evpt-fixed-session}--\eqref{eq:evpt-fixed-smart-charge}.

A compact variational form is obtained by collecting all decision variables in
\[
  z=(h,n,u,v,q,m,p^{ch},p^{dis},E,P^g,Q^g,V,\theta,\ldots)
\]
and defining the feasible set \(\mathcal{Z}\) by traffic conservation,
charging-session constraints, station limits, and power-network constraints.
The coupled equilibrium is
\begin{align}
  F(z^*)^{\top}(z-z^*)\ge 0,
  \qquad
  \forall z\in\mathcal{Z},
  \label{eq:evpt-master-vi}
\end{align}
where \(F\) contains path generalized costs, station marginal charging costs,
and power-system marginal costs or KKT residuals.

\begin{proposition}[Existence for a relaxed discretized model]
Suppose the demand profile is finite, the feasible set
\(\mathcal{Z}\) is nonempty, compact, and convex after relaxing binary plug
assignment variables, and the link-delay, queue-delay, price, and charging-cost
maps are continuous.  Then the coupled variational inequality
\eqref{eq:evpt-master-vi} has at least one solution.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The statement follows from the Hartman--Stampacchia existence theorem for a
continuous map on a nonempty compact convex feasible set.  The result is for
the relaxed time-discretized model; integer charger assignment or exact
nonconvex AC OPF can break convexity and should be handled as a later
algorithmic design issue.
\end{proof}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{System-Optimal Variant}
\label{sec:evpt-system-optimal}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The DUE model represents selfish route and station choice.  A system-optimal
planner instead minimizes total mobility, charging, and grid operating cost:
\begin{align}
  \min
  \quad
  &\sum_{k,a}
    \int_{0}^{n_{a,k}}
    \tau_{a,k}(\xi)\,d\xi
  + \sum_{s,k}
    \left[
      \lambda^P_{b(s),k} P^{EV}_{s,k}\Delta t
      + \frac{r_s}{2}(P^{EV}_{s,k})^2\Delta t
    \right] \notag\\
  &+
  \sum_{k,g} C_g(P^g_{g,k})
  + \sum_{i}\rho_i\xi_i
  + \text{queue, degradation, emission, and load-shed penalties}
  \label{eq:evpt-system-optimal-objective}
\end{align}
subject to all traffic, charging, and power constraints above.  This variant is
useful for dispatch studies, emergency control, and policy design.  It is not
equivalent to user equilibrium unless travelers face marginal congestion,
queueing, and grid-impact prices.

For implementation, the system-optimal statement must be converted into a
finite-dimensional optimization problem over the time-expanded route--charging
alternatives.  Let \(x_{w,r,k}\ge 0\) be the number of EVs of OD/class demand
block \(w\) departing in interval \(k\) and assigned to route--charging
alternative \(r\in\mathcal{R}_w\).  Let \(u_{w,k}\ge0\) be unmet travel demand,
\(P^{EV}_{s,k}\) be station charging power, \(p^{shed}_{i,k}\ge0\) be power
load shedding, and \(P^g_{g,k}\) be dispatchable generation.  The coefficients
\(A_{a,\ell}^{w,r,k}\) map an assigned vehicle to traffic-link occupancy or
inflow on road link \(a\) in interval \(\ell\), and
\(B_{s,\ell}^{w,r,k}\) map it to charging power at station \(s\) in interval
\(\ell\).  A relaxed joint dispatch can be written as
\begin{align}
  \min_{x,u,P^{EV},P^g,p^{shed}}
  \quad
  &\sum_{w,k}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_w}
    c^{tr}_{w,r,k}x_{w,r,k}
  +\sum_{s,\ell}
    \left(
      \pi_{s,\ell}P^{EV}_{s,\ell}\Delta t
      +\frac{r_s}{2}(P^{EV}_{s,\ell})^2\Delta t
    \right) \notag\\
  &+\sum_{g,\ell} C_g(P^g_{g,\ell})
  +M^{trip}\sum_{w,k}u_{w,k}
  +M^{shed}\sum_{i,\ell}p^{shed}_{i,\ell}\Delta t
  \label{eq:evpt-finite-so-objective}
\end{align}
subject to
\begin{align}
  &\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_w}x_{w,r,k}+u_{w,k}=d_{w,k},
  &&\forall w,k, \label{eq:evpt-finite-demand}\\
  &\sum_{w,k}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_w}
    A_{a,\ell}^{w,r,k}x_{w,r,k}
    \le \bar x_{a,\ell},
  &&\forall a,\ell, \label{eq:evpt-finite-road-cap}\\
  &P^{EV}_{s,\ell}
    =
    \sum_{w,k}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_w}
    B_{s,\ell}^{w,r,k}x_{w,r,k},
  &&\forall s,\ell, \label{eq:evpt-finite-station-map}\\
  &0\le P^{EV}_{s,\ell}\le\bar P^{ch}_{s,\ell},
  &&\forall s,\ell, \label{eq:evpt-finite-station-cap}\\
  &P^d_{i,\ell}
    =
    P^{base}_{i,\ell}
    +\sum_{s:b(s)=i}P^{EV}_{s,\ell}
    -p^{shed}_{i,\ell},
  &&\forall i,\ell, \label{eq:evpt-finite-load-map}\\
  &(P^g_{\ell},P^d_{\ell},V_{\ell},\theta_{\ell})
    \in\mathcal{P}_{\ell},
  &&\forall \ell. \label{eq:evpt-finite-power-set}
\end{align}
Here \(\mathcal{P}_{\ell}\) is the power-system feasible set.  It may be a
single-bus balance, a DC-OPF polytope, a LinDistFlow relaxation, or a nonlinear
AC power-flow feasible set, depending on the desired fidelity.  If
\(\mathcal{P}_{\ell}\) is linear and the quadratic terms in
\eqref{eq:evpt-finite-so-objective} are omitted or piecewise-linearly
approximated, the relaxed model is an LP.  If convex quadratic charging or
generation costs are kept, it is a convex QP under a convex power model.  If
vehicle counts, plug assignment, route activation, charger on/off decisions, or
exact AC equations are enforced, the problem becomes a MILP, MIQP, or nonconvex
MINLP.  Thus the theoretical solver for the next design step should be an LP/QP
or MILP solver over \eqref{eq:evpt-finite-so-objective}--\eqref{eq:evpt-finite-power-set},
not a shortest-path sorting rule.

\paragraph{IPOPT role.}
Although IPOPT is available through the MIPSolvers infrastructure, it is not
the right primary solver for the certified LP/MILP form above.  IPOPT solves
smooth continuous NLPs; it does not enforce integer variables and does not
return a branch-and-bound dual bound or MIP gap.  It is therefore appropriate
only after relaxing route, plug, and on/off integrality, or as a continuous
AC-OPF feasibility/refinement subproblem inside a decomposition or MINLP outer
approximation.  For the first certified joint optimizer, the preferred backend
is a linear or mixed-integer solver such as HiGHS, SCIP, or Gurobi, with IPOPT
reserved for optional nonlinear AC-power or smooth congestion refinements.

\paragraph{Algorithmic contract.}
A principled solver should assemble the matrices \(A\) and \(B\) from the
time-expanded traffic routes and charging windows, solve the finite model to
global optimality when it is LP/QP/MILP, and then decode \(x_{w,r,k}\) into
route assignments and charging sessions.  For larger instances, a supported
decomposition is also available: use a master traffic assignment in \(x\), a
station/power subproblem in \(P^{EV}\) and \(P^g\), and exchange Benders cuts or
Lagrangian prices for station and grid constraints.  In either case, the
optimality certificate is the solver's primal feasibility, dual bound, and MIP
gap rather than a locally reasonable routing order.

The implementation currently in the repository does not yet solve
\eqref{eq:evpt-finite-so-objective}--\eqref{eq:evpt-finite-power-set} at full
AC fidelity.  It now provides two distinct joint modes.  The
\texttt{CapacityAwareGreedy} mode is a deterministic feasibility baseline:
alternatives are ranked by current generalized cost, and each route receives
the largest remaining flow allowed by road residual capacity and station
residual charging power.  This heuristic has no general global-optimality
guarantee.  The \texttt{SystemOptimalLP} and \texttt{SystemOptimalMILP} modes
assemble the finite linear master problem in
\eqref{eq:evpt-finite-demand}--\eqref{eq:evpt-finite-power-set}, using route
flow variables, unmet-trip variables, road capacity rows, charging-station power
rows, and aggregate generation-capacity rows.  The LP mode uses continuous
vehicle flows; the MILP mode marks route and unmet-trip variables integer when
the input demands are integer.  Both modes expose solver certificate fields in
\texttt{EVPowerTrafficResult}: backend, status, objective, best bound, MIP gap,
and whether optimality was proven within tolerance.

The first certified implementation is deliberately linear.  Route costs are
assembled from fixed free-flow travel time, station prices, and tolls; BPR
congestion integrals, queue spillback, AC voltage constraints, and nonlinear
OPF terms remain validation or later-refinement layers.  This keeps the master
problem in the LP/MILP class where HiGHS or the native branch-and-cut backend
can provide a primal solution, dual/bound information, and a gap certificate.

\subsection{Numerical Case Study: Joint Power--Traffic Optimization}
\label{sec:evpt-numerical-case}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

This subsection records a reproducible toy case that exercises the implemented
capacity-aware greedy assignment mode.  The case is intentionally small so that
the numbers can be checked by inspection.  It is also encoded as the regression
test \texttt{EVPT: capacity-aware greedy assignment jointly respects traffic
and station capacity} in \texttt{tests/test\_ev\_power\_traffic.cpp}.

\paragraph{Network.}
One OD demand has two route/charging alternatives.  Route~1 uses a near
charging station; Route~2 uses a farther charging station.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
\hline\hline
\textbf{Route} & \textbf{Links} & \textbf{Free-flow time} &
\textbf{Station} & \textbf{Station power limit} \\
\hline
1 & \(11,12\) & \(0.5+0.5=1.0\) h & 101 & 40 kW \\
2 & \(21,22\) & \(0.8+0.7=1.5\) h & 102 & 60 kW \\
\hline\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every traffic link has capacity \(10\) veh/h, the time step is
\(\Delta t=1\) h, and link travel time uses the implemented BPR-style
evaluation
\[
  \tau_a=\tau_a^0\left[1+0.15(x_a/C_a)^4\right].
\]
The OD demand is 5 EVs departing in step 0.  Each EV requires
20 kWh at its chosen station, can charge at 20 kW, and has one time step of
dwell time.  Charging efficiency is set to 1.0 in this numerical case so the
energy accounting is transparent.  The generator capacity is 1 MW, so the
station-power decisions are far below the feeder-level generation limit; the
binding electrical limits in this case are station-level charging capacities.

\paragraph{Traffic-only assignment.}
With deterministic shortest generalized cost and no station-capacity lookahead,
all 5 EVs select Route~1 because it has the shorter free-flow travel time.  The
near station can admit only 40 kW for one hour, so it delivers 40 kWh and leaves
60 kWh of requested charging energy unserved.

\paragraph{Capacity-aware greedy assignment.}
With \texttt{AssignmentModel::CapacityAwareGreedy}, the route allocator checks
both road residual capacity and station residual charging power before
assigning vehicles.  Route~1 receives only
\[
  40\text{ kW}/20\text{ kW per EV}=2\text{ EVs},
\]
and the remaining 3 EVs are assigned to Route~2, whose 60 kW station can serve
them fully.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\hline\hline
\textbf{Policy} & \textbf{R1 EV} & \textbf{R2 EV} &
\textbf{P101} & \textbf{P102} &
\textbf{Unserved} & \textbf{Travel time} \\
\hline
Traffic-only & 5 & 0 & 40 kW & 0 kW & 60 kWh & 5.046875 h \\
Capacity-aware greedy & 2 & 3 & 40 kW & 60 kW & 0 kWh & 6.5059475 h \\
\hline\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The capacity-aware policy increases total travel time by \(1.4590725\)
vehicle-hours, but it eliminates all unserved charging energy.  This is the
intended tradeoff for a power--traffic co-optimization model: travelers are not
routed solely by road time when station or grid constraints would make the
charging plan infeasible.  In this two-route example, the greedy rule also
matches the solution of the finite LP because the cheaper route's charging
capacity is exactly saturated before the remaining demand is sent to the only
other feasible alternative.  This coincidence should not be interpreted as a
general optimality guarantee.

\paragraph{Certified LP/MILP assignment.}
With \texttt{AssignmentModel::SystemOptimalLP} or
\texttt{AssignmentModel::SystemOptimalMILP}, the same toy case is solved as a
finite optimization model.  The route-flow variables are
\[
  x_{1}= \text{EVs assigned to Route~1},\qquad
  x_{2}= \text{EVs assigned to Route~2},\qquad
  u= \text{unmet EV trips}.
\]
The demand and station-power constraints reduce to
\begin{align*}
  x_1+x_2+u &= 5,\\
  20x_1 &\le 40,\\
  20x_2 &\le 60,\\
  x_1,x_2,u &\ge 0,
\end{align*}
with \(x_1,x_2,u\in\mathbb{Z}\) in the MILP mode.  The route costs are the
linearized free-flow generalized costs \(c_1=1.0\) and \(c_2=1.5\), and the
unmet-trip penalty \(M^{trip}\) is set much larger than route costs.  Therefore
the optimizer saturates the cheaper feasible charging alternative first and
returns
\[
  x_1=2,\qquad x_2=3,\qquad u=0.
\]
The regression tests check both continuous LP and integer MILP execution,
including solver status, objective, best bound, and MIP gap fields.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\hline\hline
\textbf{Step} & \textbf{P101} & \textbf{P102} &
\textbf{EV load} & \textbf{Generation margin} \\
\hline
0 & 0 kW & 0 kW & 0.00 MW & 1.00 MW \\
1 & 40 kW & 0 kW & 0.04 MW & 0.96 MW \\
2 & 0 kW & 60 kW & 0.06 MW & 0.94 MW \\
3 & 0 kW & 0 kW & 0.00 MW & 1.00 MW \\
\hline\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The traffic-only solution is infeasible under
\texttt{allow\_unserved\_charging\_energy=false}; the joint solution is
feasible with 100 kWh requested and 100 kWh delivered.

\subsection{Minimal Theoretical Contract for the Next Design Step}
\label{sec:evpt-next-contract}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The theory above implies the following minimum extension contract:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A traffic graph must provide directed links, length, free-flow time,
        capacity, and optional jam accumulation.
  \item Time-varying OD demand must be indexed by departure interval, OD pair,
        and EV class.
  \item A route alternative must encode road path, station sequence, requested
        energy, and admissible charging windows.
  \item Dynamic traffic assignment must output station arrival rates and
        requested charging energy by station and time.
  \item Charging sessions must be generated from station arrivals and must
        carry arrival time, departure time, initial energy, target energy,
        charger limit, efficiency, and V2G capability.
  \item Smart charging must update \texttt{ChargingStation::p\_total\_kw} and
        \texttt{q\_total\_kvar} for each time step before PF/OPF assembly.
  \item The power solve must return feasibility diagnostics and, when OPF is
        used, bus-level marginal prices or equivalent charging signals.
  \item The coupled simulator must report whether it solved a DUE fixed point,
        a stochastic assignment fixed point, a certified LP/QP/MILP
        system-optimal dispatch, or a heuristic feasibility policy.
\end{enumerate}

Until the traffic and session objects exist in code, the mathematically valid
bridge to the current infrastructure is to treat \(P^{EV}_{s,k}\) as a
time-series charging-station load profile and run the existing time-series PF
or OPF machinery.  The full coupled model begins when
\(P^{EV}_{s,k}\) is produced endogenously by
\eqref{eq:evpt-fixed-dta}--\eqref{eq:evpt-fixed-smart-charge}.

\subsection{Implemented Module Boundary}
\label{sec:evpt-implemented-module-boundary}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The first code-level module is wired at the same architectural level as
\texttt{power\_flow}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item public headers:
        \texttt{include/hacdcpf/ev\_power\_traffic.hpp} and
        \texttt{include/hacdcpf/ev\_power\_traffic/ev\_power\_traffic\_simulation.hpp},
  \item implementation:
        \texttt{src/ev\_power\_traffic/ev\_power\_traffic\_simulation.cpp},
  \item CMake source registration in the core \texttt{hacdcpf} library,
  \item regression tests in \texttt{tests/test\_ev\_power\_traffic.cpp}.
\end{itemize}

The implemented entry point is
\[
  \texttt{evpt::simulate\_ev\_power\_traffic(problem, options)}.
\]
It implements the discretized coupling skeleton used by this chapter:
time-indexed road capacity and blockage, deterministic or logit route
assignment over explicit route alternatives, capacity-aware greedy assignment,
certified system-optimal LP/MILP assignment, unmet-demand accounting, charging
session synthesis, station-level charging/V2G dispatch, station load write-back to
\texttt{ChargingStation::p\_total\_kw}/\texttt{q\_total\_kvar}, and
generation-capacity plus optional power-flow validation.

The first implementation is intentionally not yet a full continuous DUE solver
or nonlinear ACOPF/LMP-coupled optimizer.  It provides a tested module boundary
and state/result contract on which the later algorithmic design can add:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item link-transmission queue propagation across multiple road cells,
  \item fixed-point DUE or stochastic DTA iterations,
  \item convex QP costs and piecewise-linear congestion approximations,
  \item OPF-derived station prices \(\lambda^P_{b(s),k}\),
  \item exact charger-plug assignment and queue discipline,
  \item DC-side charging stations and converter-aware dispatch.
\end{enumerate}

The current regression tests verify the three modelling choices that are most
important for the next design step:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a blocked road link produces unmet travel demand unless the option
        \texttt{allow\_unserved\_travel\_demand} is enabled,
  \item a V2G-capable plugged-in session can make
        \(P^{EV}_{s,k}<0\), i.e. reverse station power,
  \item accepted trips generate station load profiles that are written back to
        the existing power-system data model and checked against generation
        capacity.
  \item the LP/MILP joint optimizer returns the same feasible 2/3 route split
        in the toy case and exposes solver certificate fields.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cell Transmission Model for Dynamic Traffic Flow}
\label{sec:evpt-ctm}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The fixed-cost assignment used in the current implementation treats link
traversal time as independent of loading.  Section~\ref{sec:evpt-dta-flow}
already states the link-transmission sending and receiving functions; this
subsection gives the complete discretization and shows it yields a linear
programme.

\subsubsection{Cell Discretization}
\label{sec:evpt-ctm-cells}

For each directed road link \(a\in\mathcal{A}^{tr}\), the triangular
fundamental diagram is parameterized by three constants:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline\hline
\textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Unit} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\(v_f^a\)      & km/h  & free-flow speed \\
\(w^a\)        & km/h  & backward (congestion) wave speed, \(w^a>0\) \\
\(q_{\max}^a\) & veh/h & capacity flow at the critical density \\
\hline\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The CFL-stable cell length is \(\delta_a = v_f^a \Delta t\) (km), and the
number of cells is \(M_a = \lceil L_a/\delta_a \rceil\), where \(L_a\) is the
link length.  The jam accumulation per cell is
\[
  N_j^a = q_{\max}^a\Delta t\,\frac{v_f^a+w^a}{w^a}.
\]
The per-step capacity (vehicles that can cross a cell boundary in \(\Delta t\))
is
\[
  Q^a = q_{\max}^a\Delta t.
\]

\subsubsection{State Variables and Transition Flows}
\label{sec:evpt-ctm-vars}

For cell \(m\in\{1,\ldots,M_a\}\) and time step \(k\in\mathcal{T}\), let
\(n_{a,m,k}\ge 0\) be the vehicle accumulation.  The inter-cell flow
\(y_{a,m,k}\ge 0\) denotes vehicles moving from cell \(m\) to cell \(m+1\)
(or out of the last cell) during step \(k\).  Define the \emph{sending} and
\emph{receiving} capacities at step \(k\):
\begin{align}
  S_{a,m,k}
  &= \min\!\left(n_{a,m,k},\; Q^a\right),
  \label{eq:ctm-sending}\\
  R_{a,m,k}
  &= \min\!\left(\frac{w^a}{v_f^a}\bigl(N_j^a - n_{a,m,k}\bigr),\; Q^a\right).
  \label{eq:ctm-receiving}
\end{align}
The CTM transition flow satisfies
\[
  y_{a,m,k} = \min\!\left(S_{a,m,k},\; R_{a,m+1,k}\right),
\]
with the convention \(R_{a,M_a+1,k}=Q^a\) (free exit at the downstream
intersection) and \(S_{a,0,k}=y_{a,0,k}\) (exogenous inflow to be coupled to
route assignment).

\subsubsection{LP Representation of the CTM min-Constraint}
\label{sec:evpt-ctm-lp}

The pointwise minimum \(\min(S,R)\) is \emph{not} directly linear, but in a
system-optimal framework the social-welfare objective naturally drives
inter-cell flows to their maximum feasible value; the $\min$ therefore
decomposes into two upper bounds:
\begin{align}
  y_{a,m,k}
  &\le S_{a,m,k}
  = \min\!\left(n_{a,m,k},\;Q^a\right), \label{eq:ctm-ub-send}\\
  y_{a,m,k}
  &\le R_{a,m+1,k}
  = \min\!\left(\tfrac{w^a}{v_f^a}(N_j^a-n_{a,m+1,k}),\;Q^a\right).
  \label{eq:ctm-ub-recv}
\end{align}
Each of these \(\min(\cdot,\cdot)\) upper bounds is itself LP-representable
using two separate linear inequalities:
\begin{align}
  y_{a,m,k} &\le n_{a,m,k},           \label{eq:ctm-lp-s1}\\
  y_{a,m,k} &\le Q^a,                 \label{eq:ctm-lp-s2}\\
  y_{a,m,k} &\le \frac{w^a}{v_f^a}\bigl(N_j^a-n_{a,m+1,k}\bigr),
                                       \label{eq:ctm-lp-r1}\\
  y_{a,m,k} &\le Q^a,                 \label{eq:ctm-lp-r2}\\
  y_{a,m,k} &\ge 0.                   \label{eq:ctm-lp-nn}
\end{align}
The state update is linear by construction:
\begin{align}
  n_{a,m,k+1}
  &= n_{a,m,k} + y_{a,m-1,k} - y_{a,m,k},
  &&\forall a,m,k. \label{eq:ctm-state-update}\\
  0 &\le n_{a,m,k} \le N_j^a,
  &&\forall a,m,k. \label{eq:ctm-jam-bound}
\end{align}

\begin{remark}[Validity of the LP relaxation]
The LP relaxation of the CTM min-constraint is exact for system-optimal
assignment because the objective strictly rewards moving vehicles forward.  At
any LP optimal solution, every flow \(y_{a,m,k}\) that is below both
\(S_{a,m,k}\) and \(R_{a,m+1,k}\) can be increased without violating any
constraint, strictly improving the objective.  Hence the LP optimal always
satisfies \(y_{a,m,k}=\min(S_{a,m,k},R_{a,m+1,k})\) with equality, reproducing
exact CTM dynamics.  This argument does not hold for user-equilibrium
assignment, where the objective is not monotone in throughput.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Road Failure in CTM}
\label{sec:evpt-ctm-failure}

A full link failure at step \(k_0\) for steps \(k\in[k_0,k_0+T_f]\) is
modelled by setting
\[
  Q^a_k = 0, \qquad k\in[k_0,k_0+T_f],
\]
which forces \(y_{a,m,k}=0\) for all \(m\), preventing any vehicle from
traversing link \(a\) during the failure window.  Vehicles already inside
the link remain in their cells (spillback is governed by upstream receiving
functions), and downstream queues form naturally through the CTM dynamics.
Partial capacity reduction (e.g., accident-induced lane closure) is
modelled by replacing \(Q^a\) with \(\sigma Q^a\) for \(\sigma\in(0,1)\).
No structural change to the LP is required in either case.

\subsubsection{Travel Time from CTM}
\label{sec:evpt-ctm-tt}

The total vehicle-time in the network (sum of all cell accumulations over time)
is a linear function of the state variables:
\begin{align}
  T^{\text{total}}
  &= \sum_{a}\sum_{m=1}^{M_a}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{T}}
    n_{a,m,k}\,\Delta t.
  \label{eq:ctm-total-tt}
\end{align}
Multiplying by the value-of-time \(\omega_T\) gives the traffic delay cost used
in the social-welfare objective.  This is strictly linear in \(n_{a,m,k}\).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Single-LP Joint CTM--DC-OPF Formulation}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-lp}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The iterative LMP-price decomposition in
\eqref{eq:evpt-fixed-dta}--\eqref{eq:evpt-fixed-power} is an approximation
algorithm.  As shown in Section~\ref{sec:evpt-system-optimal}, when the
power-system model is a DC-OPF polytope and traffic dynamics follow CTM, the
joint problem \eqref{eq:evpt-finite-so-objective}--\eqref{eq:evpt-finite-power-set}
is a single LP.  This subsection states that LP completely.

\subsubsection{Decision Variables}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-vars}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline\hline
\textbf{Variable} & \textbf{Domain} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\hline
\(x_{w,r,k}\ge0\)     & vehicles & EVs of demand \(w\) departing step \(k\) on
                                    route/charging alternative \(r\) \\
\(u_{w,k}\ge0\)       & vehicles & unmet travel demand \\
\(n_{a,m,k}\ge0\)     & vehicles & CTM cell accumulation \\
\(y_{a,m,k}\ge0\)     & vehicles & CTM inter-cell flow \\
\(P^{EV}_{s,k}\ge0\)  & kW       & station \(s\) net charging power at step \(k\) \\
\(P^g_{g,k}\ge0\)     & MW       & dispatch of generator \(g\) at step \(k\) \\
\(\theta_{i,k}\)      & rad      & DC voltage angle at bus \(i\), step \(k\) \\
\(p^{shed}_{i,k}\ge0\)& MW       & load shedding at bus \(i\), step \(k\) \\
\hline\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Objective: Maximise Social Welfare}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-obj}

\begin{align}
  \max\quad
  &\underbrace{
    \sum_{w,r,k} \mathrm{WTP}_w\, x_{w,r,k}
  }_{\text{EV gross benefit}}
  -\underbrace{
    \omega_T\sum_{a,m,k} n_{a,m,k}\,\Delta t
  }_{\text{traffic delay cost}}
  -\underbrace{
    \sum_{g,k} C_g\!\left(P^g_{g,k}\right)\Delta t
  }_{\text{generation cost}}
  -\underbrace{
    M^{trip}\sum_{w,k} u_{w,k}
  }_{\text{unmet-demand penalty}}
  -\underbrace{
    M^{shed}\sum_{i,k} p^{shed}_{i,k}\,\Delta t
  }_{\text{load-shed penalty}}
  \label{eq:joint-lp-obj}
\end{align}

\(\mathrm{WTP}_w\) is the willingness-to-pay per vehicle of demand block \(w\),
\(\omega_T\) is the value of time (\$/vehicle-hour), and \(C_g(\cdot)\) is the
generator cost function (piecewise-linear or linear in MW).  \(M^{trip}\) and
\(M^{shed}\) are large penalties that enforce demand satisfaction and grid
feasibility as soft constraints.

\subsubsection{Traffic Constraints}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-traffic}

\paragraph{Demand conservation.}
\begin{align}
  \sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_w} x_{w,r,k} + u_{w,k}
  &= d_{w,k},
  &&\forall w,k. \label{eq:jlp-demand}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Route-to-CTM inflow coupling.}
Let \(\alpha_{a,\ell}^{w,r,k_0}\in\{0,1\}\) be the time-expanded incidence
coefficient equal to 1 if vehicles departing step \(k_0\) on alternative
\(r\in\mathcal{R}_w\) enter link \(a\) at step \(\ell\) (computed from
free-flow propagation times before the LP is assembled):
\begin{align}
  y_{a,0,\ell}
  &= \sum_{w}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_w}\sum_{k_0}
    \alpha_{a,\ell}^{w,r,k_0}\,x_{w,r,k_0},
  &&\forall a,\ell. \label{eq:jlp-route-ctm}
\end{align}

\paragraph{CTM dynamics.}
Equations
\eqref{eq:ctm-state-update}--\eqref{eq:ctm-jam-bound} and
\eqref{eq:ctm-lp-s1}--\eqref{eq:ctm-lp-nn} for all links, cells, and steps.

\paragraph{Road failure.}
For each failed link \(a\) during \([k_0,k_0+T_f]\):
\begin{align}
  y_{a,m,k} &= 0,
  &&\forall m,\ k\in[k_0,k_0+T_f]. \label{eq:jlp-failure}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Charging and Power-Coupling Constraints}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-power}

\paragraph{Route-to-station energy coupling.}
Let \(\beta_{s,k}^{w,r,k_0}\ge0\) be the per-vehicle charging rate (kW) that
vehicles from \((w,r,k_0)\) contribute to station \(s\) at step \(k\) (the
product of requested energy per vehicle divided by the dwell duration in steps):
\begin{align}
  P^{EV}_{s,k}
  &= \sum_{w}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_w}\sum_{k_0}
    \beta_{s,k}^{w,r,k_0}\,x_{w,r,k_0},
  &&\forall s,k. \label{eq:jlp-ev-load}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Station capacity.}
\begin{align}
  P^{EV}_{s,k} &\le \overline P^{ch}_s,
  &&\forall s,k. \label{eq:jlp-station-cap}
\end{align}

\paragraph{DC-OPF bus load.}
\begin{align}
  P^d_{i,k}
  &= P^{base}_{i,k}
    + 10^{-3}\sum_{s:\,b(s)=i} P^{EV}_{s,k}
    - p^{shed}_{i,k},
  &&\forall i,k. \label{eq:jlp-busload}
\end{align}

\paragraph{DC power balance and branch flows.}
Let \(B\) be the network susceptance (B-\(\theta\)) matrix and
\(H\) the branch-bus incidence matrix:
\begin{align}
  \sum_{g:\,g(g)=i} P^g_{g,k} - P^d_{i,k}
  &= [B\theta_k]_i,
  &&\forall i,k, \label{eq:jlp-pbalance}\\
  \left|[H\theta_k]_\ell\right|
  &\le \overline P_\ell,
  &&\forall \ell,k. \label{eq:jlp-branch-lim}\\
  \theta_{ref,k} &= 0,
  &&\forall k. \label{eq:jlp-slack}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Generator limits and cost linearisation.}
\begin{align}
  \underline P^g_g \le P^g_{g,k}
  &\le \overline P^g_g,
  &&\forall g,k. \label{eq:jlp-gen-lim}
\end{align}
The quadratic cost \(C_g(P)=c_{2,g}P^2+c_{1,g}P+c_{0,g}\) is replaced by a
piecewise-linear (PWL) approximation with \(K\) break-points.  Introducing
auxiliary variables \(f^{(l)}_{g,k}\ge0\) for segment \(l\):
\begin{align}
  P^g_{g,k}
  &= \sum_{l=1}^{K} f^{(l)}_{g,k}, \label{eq:jlp-pwl-sum}\\
  0 \le f^{(l)}_{g,k}
  &\le \Delta P^{(l)}_g,
  &&\forall l, \label{eq:jlp-pwl-seg}\\
  C_g(P^g_{g,k})
  &\approx c_{1,g}^{(l)}\text{-weighted sum of }f^{(l)}_{g,k}.
  \label{eq:jlp-pwl-cost}
\end{align}
For linear costs (\(c_{2,g}=0\)), the PWL approximation is exact with \(K=1\).

\subsubsection{Complete LP Statement}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-lp-full}

The joint social-welfare maximisation problem is the LP:
\begin{align}
  \max\quad
  &\eqref{eq:joint-lp-obj} \notag\\
  \text{s.t.}\quad
  &\eqref{eq:jlp-demand},
  \eqref{eq:jlp-route-ctm},
  \eqref{eq:ctm-state-update}--\eqref{eq:ctm-lp-nn},
  \eqref{eq:jlp-failure},
  \eqref{eq:jlp-ev-load}--\eqref{eq:jlp-busload},
  \eqref{eq:jlp-pbalance}--\eqref{eq:jlp-pwl-cost},\\
  &x_{w,r,k},\,u_{w,k},\,n_{a,m,k},\,y_{a,m,k},\,
   P^{EV}_{s,k},\,P^g_{g,k},\,p^{shed}_{i,k}\ge0,
   \quad\theta_{i,k}\text{ free}.
  \label{eq:joint-lp-full}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Convergence and Optimality Guarantees}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-convergence}

\begin{theorem}[Global optimality of the single-LP formulation]
\label{thm:joint-lp-global}
Suppose the generator cost functions are piecewise-linear and convex, the
fundamental diagram is triangular, and the power network is represented by a
DC power-flow model.  Then the joint social-welfare maximisation problem
\eqref{eq:joint-lp-full} is a linear programme.  Any primal-feasible
dual-feasible solution returned by an LP solver (e.g.\ HiGHS simplex or
interior-point) is a global optimum, and strong duality certifies the optimality
gap is zero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
All constraint sets are polyhedral (linear equalities and inequalities).  The
objective \eqref{eq:joint-lp-obj} is linear after the PWL cost substitution
\eqref{eq:jlp-pwl-sum}--\eqref{eq:jlp-pwl-cost}.  The LP relaxation of
the CTM \(\min\)-constraint is exact for system-optimal assignment by the
monotone-throughput argument in Remark~\ref{sec:evpt-ctm-lp}.  Thus the
problem is an LP over a non-empty bounded polyhedron (boundedness follows from
finite demand, jam capacities, and generator limits).  By the LP strong
duality theorem, the optimal primal value equals the optimal dual value, and
any primal-feasible dual-feasible solution achieves this value.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Failure of the iterative decomposition]
\label{cor:iterative-failure}
The iterative LMP-price decomposition in
\texttt{solve\_joint\_social\_welfare()} is a fixed-point iteration on two LP
subproblems.  It does not in general converge to the global optimum of
\eqref{eq:joint-lp-full} because:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The LP optimal route assignment \(x^*({\pi})\) is a piecewise-constant
        (step) function of the station prices \(\pi\), so the LMP map
        \(\pi\mapsto\lambda^{LMP}(x^*(\pi))\) is discontinuous wherever a
        threshold price causes all vehicles to switch routes.
  \item A fixed step size \(\alpha\) provides no convergence guarantee when
        the Lipschitz constant of the LMP map exceeds \(1/\alpha\).
  \item Multiple fixed points (Nash price equilibria) may exist; which one
        is reached depends entirely on the initial price guess.
\end{enumerate}
The single-LP formulation \eqref{eq:joint-lp-full} eliminates all three
failure modes by internalising traffic and power coupling within one solver
call.
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Complexity.}
The LP has
\(O(|\mathcal{W}||\mathcal{R}|N_T + \sum_a M_a N_T + |\mathcal{S}|N_T +
|\mathcal{G}|N_T + |\mathcal{B}|N_T)\) variables and a comparable number of
constraints.  For a city-scale network with 500 links, 5 cells per link,
100 OD pairs, 20 routes each, and \(N_T=24\) steps, this is approximately
\(3\times10^5\) variables — well within HiGHS's practical range.

\subsubsection{Dual Variables and LMP Interpretation}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-duals}

The dual variables of the joint LP \eqref{eq:joint-lp-full} have direct
economic interpretations:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline\hline
\textbf{Primal constraint} & \textbf{Dual variable} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
\hline
\eqref{eq:jlp-demand}   & \(\mu_{w,k}\)       & shadow price of OD demand (optimal toll) \\
\eqref{eq:jlp-failure}  & \(\nu_{a,k}\)       & value of restoring failed link \(a\) at step \(k\) \\
\eqref{eq:jlp-station-cap} & \(\rho_{s,k}\)   & station congestion price (\$/kW) \\
\eqref{eq:jlp-pbalance} & \(\lambda^P_{i,k}\) & DC-OPF locational marginal price (\$/MWh) \\
\eqref{eq:jlp-branch-lim} & \(\sigma_{\ell,k}\) & branch congestion rent \\
\eqref{eq:ctm-jam-bound} & \(\zeta_{a,m,k}\) & shadow cost of cell space (congestion externality) \\
\hline\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The dual variable \(\lambda^P_{i,k}\) is the LMP that the iterative
decomposition tries to approximate through successive substitution.  In the
single-LP solution, \(\lambda^P_{i,k}\) is returned directly by the LP dual
and is \emph{consistent} with the route assignment and CTM dynamics — a
property the iterative method cannot guarantee at a non-converged iterate.

\subsubsection{Relation to the Implemented Code}
\label{sec:evpt-joint-code-relation}

The function \texttt{solve\_joint\_social\_welfare()} in
\texttt{src/ev\_power\_traffic/joint\_social\_welfare.cpp} implements the
iterative decomposition described in Corollary~\ref{cor:iterative-failure}.
It is a valid heuristic for lightly loaded, uncongested networks (where the LMP
map is nearly constant and the fixed-point is unique), but it does not provide
an optimality certificate for congested or failure scenarios.

The single-LP formulation \eqref{eq:joint-lp-full} is the target design for
the next implementation step.  It requires assembling the time-expanded
matrices \(\alpha_{a,\ell}^{w,r,k_0}\) and \(\beta_{s,k}^{w,r,k_0}\) from the
route alternatives, adding CTM cell variables to the existing LP builder in
\texttt{AssignmentModel::SystemOptimalLP}, and augmenting the constraint matrix
with the DC power-balance rows
\eqref{eq:jlp-pbalance}--\eqref{eq:jlp-branch-lim}.  All of these operations
are linear; the solver backend (HiGHS) does not change.  The dual solution
provides LMPs, station congestion prices, and link restoration values as
by-products of the single solve, replacing the separate DC-OPF calls in the
iterative algorithm.

\subsection{Temporal Discrepancy: Traffic Time as State vs.\ Power Time as Parameter}
\label{sec:evpt-time-discrepancy}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{Source of the Discrepancy}
\label{sec:evpt-time-source}

The two physical subsystems treat time in fundamentally different ways.

\paragraph{Power-system time: exogenous parameter.}
The DC-OPF is a static optimisation problem solved independently at each
pre-fixed interval $k\in\mathcal{T}=\{0,1,\ldots,N_T\}$.  The interval
boundaries $t_k = k\Delta t^{\mathrm{pow}}$ are chosen by the analyst and do
not depend on the network state.  Load, generation limits, and prices are
functions of $k$; the power equations carry no memory between steps unless a
storage or ramp constraint explicitly links $P_{g,k}$ and $P_{g,k+1}$.

\paragraph{Traffic time: endogenous state variable.}
In dynamic traffic theory, the arrival time $\theta_s$ of a vehicle at
charging station $s$ is \emph{not} an input — it is the solution to the
propagation equations
\begin{align}
  \theta_{\ell+1}
  &= \theta_\ell + \tau_{a_\ell,\lfloor\theta_\ell/\Delta t\rfloor},
  \label{eq:disc-arrival-recursion}
\end{align}
where $\tau_{a,k}$ itself depends on the link loading $n_{a,k}$ at the time of
traversal.  At the PDE level, $\theta$ satisfies the Hamilton--Jacobi equation
\begin{align}
  \frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial t}
  + \frac{f(\rho(x,t))}{\rho(x,t)}\frac{\partial\theta^*}{\partial x}
  = 1,
  \label{eq:disc-hj}
\end{align}
whose characteristics are the trajectories of individual vehicles.  Time
\emph{appears as the dependent variable} of this equation, not as the
independent parameter.

\paragraph{The coupling tension.}
The charging load $P^{EV}_{s,k}$ that enters the power-system constraints
\eqref{eq:jlp-ev-load} must be indexed by the power time step $k$.  But the
time at which a vehicle arrives at station $s$ is determined by
\eqref{eq:disc-arrival-recursion} — which depends on the traffic state, which
depends on the route assignment, which depends on the charging prices
$\pi_{s,k}$.  The coupling is therefore circular: the power load depends on
traffic dynamics, and traffic dynamics depend on the power prices that are
outputs of the OPF with that load.  Breaking this circle requires a principled
treatment of the temporal mismatch.

\subsubsection{Three Coupling Regimes}
\label{sec:evpt-time-regimes}

Three qualitatively different situations arise depending on how traffic and
power time grids are aligned.

\paragraph{Regime 1: Shared grid, free-flow pre-computation (current code).}
Both subsystems use the same step size $\Delta t$.  Arrival times are
pre-computed from free-flow travel times before the LP is assembled:
\begin{align}
  \hat\theta_s^{w,r,k_0}
  &= k_0\Delta t
    + \sum_{a\in\text{road path of }r\text{ before }s} \tau_a^0,
  \label{eq:disc-freeflow}
\end{align}
and the coupling coefficient is set to a fixed incidence:
\begin{align}
  \beta_{s,k}^{w,r,k_0}
  &=
  \begin{cases}
    e_{w,r,s}^{\mathrm{req}}/\Delta t^{\mathrm{dwell}},
    & k = \lfloor \hat\theta_s^{w,r,k_0}/\Delta t \rfloor, \\
    0, & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:disc-freeflow-beta}
\end{align}
This converts the state variable $\theta_s$ into a parameter and makes
$\beta_{s,k}^{w,r,k_0}$ a pre-assembled constant matrix.  The LP is then
well-defined.  The limitation is that $\beta$ does not adjust when congestion
extends the actual arrival time beyond the free-flow estimate, so the OPF
load profile can be wrong by up to one or more steps under heavy traffic.

\paragraph{Regime 2: Shared grid, CTM-based endogenous coupling.}
The CTM operates on the same step size $\Delta t$ as the OPF.  The
arrival-time state variable $\theta_s$ is implicitly encoded in the exit flows
of the last CTM cell on each link entering station $s$.  Specifically, if link
$a_s$ is the approach link to station $s$ with $M_{a_s}$ cells, then
$y_{a_s,M_{a_s},k}$ is the number of vehicles entering station $s$ during
step $k$.  The coupling constraint \eqref{eq:jlp-ev-load} becomes
\begin{align}
  P^{EV}_{s,k}
  &= p_{\mathrm{ch}}^{\mathrm{veh}} \cdot \Delta t
    \sum_{a\to s}
    y_{a,M_a,k},
  &&\forall s,k,
  \label{eq:disc-ctm-coupling}
\end{align}
where $p_{\mathrm{ch}}^{\mathrm{veh}}$ is the per-vehicle charging rate (kW)
and the sum is over all approach links to station $s$.  The coupling
coefficient $\beta$ is no longer pre-fixed; it is determined endogenously by
the CTM exit flows within the same LP solve.  The arrival-time state variable
is thereby \emph{absorbed into the LP variables} $y_{a,m,k}$, and no
pre-computation is required.  This is the key advantage of the CTM over
static-with-BPR models for the joint formulation.

\begin{remark}[Resolution of the temporal discrepancy in Regime 2]
In Regime 2, both the traffic and power subproblems are indexed on the same
discrete grid $\{0,\ldots,N_T\}$ with the same step $\Delta t$.  The CTM
propagates vehicle arrivals forward through time using the same index $k$ as
the OPF.  The continuous arrival-time state variable $\theta_s$ is replaced by
the discrete occupancy and flow variables $(n_{a,m,k}, y_{a,m,k})$, which are
LP decision variables.  The temporal discrepancy is therefore resolved by
discretization on a shared grid: the Hamilton--Jacobi equation
\eqref{eq:disc-hj} is approximated by the CTM finite-volume scheme
\eqref{eq:ctm-state-update}, and both domains use the same time index.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Regime 3: Multi-rate grids (traffic finer than power).}
The CFL stability condition for CTM requires
\begin{align}
  \Delta t^{\mathrm{tr}} \le \frac{\delta_a}{v_f^a},
  \label{eq:disc-cfl}
\end{align}
where $\delta_a = L_a/M_a$ is the cell length.  For urban links of length
$\delta_a = 0.5\,\mathrm{km}$ and $v_f^a = 60\,\mathrm{km/h}$, this gives
$\Delta t^{\mathrm{tr}} \le 30\,\mathrm{s}$.  Power systems typically use
$\Delta t^{\mathrm{pow}} = 15\,\mathrm{min}$ or $1\,\mathrm{h}$.  The ratio
$R = \Delta t^{\mathrm{pow}}/\Delta t^{\mathrm{tr}}$ can therefore be
$30$--$120$.

In this regime, traffic is simulated on a fine grid $k^{tr}\in\{0,\ldots,RN_T\}$
and the OPF runs on a coarse grid $K\in\{0,\ldots,N_T\}$.  The coupling is
through a temporal aggregation operator:
\begin{align}
  P^{EV}_{s,K}
  &= \frac{1}{R}\sum_{k^{tr}=KR}^{(K+1)R-1}
    \hat P^{EV}_{s,k^{tr}},
  &&\forall s,K,
  \label{eq:disc-aggregation}
\end{align}
where $\hat P^{EV}_{s,k^{tr}}$ is the instantaneous charging load from the
CTM on the fine grid.  This aggregation is a linear constraint.  The joint LP
retains its structure; the only change is that the traffic block runs on the
finer index $k^{tr}$ while the OPF block runs on $K$, and
\eqref{eq:disc-aggregation} links them.

\subsubsection{Formal Unification via the Time-Expanded Network}
\label{sec:evpt-time-ten}

All three regimes can be unified in the \emph{time-expanded network} (TEN)
framework.  Replace each spatial node $i$ and link $a$ by node copies
$(i,k)$ and link copies $(a,k)$ for each time step $k$.  A vehicle present in
cell $(a,m)$ at time $k$ occupies node $(a,m,k)$ in the TEN.  The CTM
state-update equation \eqref{eq:ctm-state-update} is then a simple
conservation-of-flow equation on the TEN arc $((a,m,k),(a,m,k+1))$.

In this representation, the traffic subsystem is a network-flow LP over the
TEN, and the power subsystem is an LP over the bus-time product
$\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{T}$.  The coupling constraint \eqref{eq:disc-ctm-coupling}
or \eqref{eq:disc-aggregation} adds arcs from TEN exit nodes to bus-time
nodes.  The full joint problem is therefore a single LP on the product graph
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{joint}}
  = \mathcal{G}^{tr}\times\mathcal{T}^{tr}
    \cup
    \mathcal{G}^{pow}\times\mathcal{T}^{pow}
    \cup
    \mathcal{E}^{coupling},
  \label{eq:disc-joint-graph}
\end{align}
and the temporal discrepancy reduces to the choice of $\mathcal{T}^{tr}$ and
$\mathcal{T}^{pow}$ and the structure of the coupling edges
$\mathcal{E}^{coupling}$.

\subsubsection{Implication for Step Size Selection}
\label{sec:evpt-time-step}

A practical co-design rule follows from the three regimes.  Define the
\emph{minimum traversal time} of any link:
\[
  \Delta t^{\min} = \min_{a\in\mathcal{A}^{tr}} \frac{L_a}{v_f^a}.
\]
Three step-size choices are natural:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Traffic step} $\Delta t^{tr} \le \Delta t^{\min}$ — satisfies
        CFL for all links with one cell per link.  For urban networks with
        short links ($L_a\sim 0.3\,\mathrm{km}$, $v_f^a=50\,\mathrm{km/h}$),
        this gives $\Delta t^{tr}\le 22\,\mathrm{s}$.

  \item \textbf{Power step} $\Delta t^{\mathrm{pow}}$ — set by the power
        operator's scheduling resolution.  Intra-hour markets use
        $\Delta t^{\mathrm{pow}}=5$--$60\,\mathrm{min}$.

  \item \textbf{Charging dwell step} $\Delta t^{\mathrm{dwell}}$ — the
        duration a vehicle occupies a charger plug.  For DC fast charging at
        100 kW and a 50 kWh battery, $\Delta t^{\mathrm{dwell}}\approx 30\,\mathrm{min}$.
\end{enumerate}

In Regime 2 (shared grid), all three must be aligned:
$\Delta t = \Delta t^{tr} = \Delta t^{\mathrm{pow}} = \Delta t^{\mathrm{dwell}}$.
This forces a very fine power step, which may be unnecessarily expensive for
the OPF.  In Regime 3 (multi-rate), traffic uses $\Delta t^{tr}$, power uses
$\Delta t^{\mathrm{pow}} = R\Delta t^{tr}$, and the charging dwell is a
multiple of $\Delta t^{tr}$.  This is the physically correct and
computationally tractable design for real networks.

\subsubsection{Summary}
\label{sec:evpt-time-summary}

The temporal discrepancy between traffic and power systems arises because
travel time is a state variable (governed by a Hamilton--Jacobi PDE with time
as the dependent variable) while power-system time is a fixed scheduling
parameter.  The discrepancy is resolved in three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Discretization}: the CTM converts the continuous travel-time
        PDE into a linear finite-volume scheme on a discrete time grid, making
        the arrival-time distribution an LP variable rather than an analytical
        expression.

  \item \textbf{Endogenous coupling}: in the single-LP formulation
        \eqref{eq:joint-lp-full}, the coupling matrix $\beta$ is replaced by
        CTM exit-flow constraints \eqref{eq:disc-ctm-coupling}, so the OPF
        load profile adjusts endogenously to the traffic state within the same
        LP solve.

  \item \textbf{Multi-rate aggregation}: when the CFL step is finer than the
        OPF scheduling resolution, the temporal aggregation operator
        \eqref{eq:disc-aggregation} maps fine-grid charging loads to
        coarse-grid OPF intervals as a linear constraint, preserving the LP
        structure and global optimality.
\end{enumerate}

The current implementation (Regime 1) uses pre-fixed $\beta$ coefficients
computed from free-flow travel times.  It is exact when the network is
uncongested and $\Delta t$ is large enough to cover the full route traversal.
The CTM-based formulation (Regime 2 or 3) is the correct target for congested
or time-critical scenarios.
